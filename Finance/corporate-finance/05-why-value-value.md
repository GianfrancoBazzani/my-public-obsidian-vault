# Why Value Value?

## Definition

The goal of a company is to maximize its **long-term value** for the shareholders. The value of the company is the present value of its future free cash flows, discounted at the WACC (see [Companies and Corporate Finance](00-companies-and-corporate-finance.md)). This value belongs to all the providers of funds, the creditors and the shareholders. The value for the shareholders is the value of the company minus the value of the debt. If the value of the debt does not change, each increase in the value of the company goes to the shareholders. The company creates value when its return is higher than its cost of capital (see [Value Creation](04-value-creation.md)).

The long term is already in the objective of [Companies and Corporate Finance](00-companies-and-corporate-finance.md). The sum includes the discounted free cash flows of all the periods, to $t = \infty$.

$$\max_{p,q,I,f} \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \frac{\Pi_t}{(1+\kappa_t)^t}$$

A short-term focus maximizes only the first terms of this sum. It can increase $\Pi_1$ and reduce all the $\Pi_t$ that come after it (see the example below).

This goal is different from three other goals that look similar.

- The **share price today**, which depends on the expectations of the investors.
- The **accounting profit**, which ignores the cost of capital and the investment.
- The **growth** of the company, which creates value only in some conditions.

The shareholders are the **residual claimants** of the company. They get what remains after the company pays the employees, the suppliers, the creditors and the taxes. For this reason the long-term value for the shareholders also needs the support of the other groups.

## The advantages of a long-term view

**For the companies**

- **Better investment decisions.** The company keeps the investments that pay back only after some years, e.g. research and development (R&D), the training of the employees and the brand.
- **A higher return on capital.** The company accepts the projects with $NPV > 0$ (see [Investment Decisions](02-investment-decisions.md)), not the projects that improve the results of the next quarter.
- **More stable results.** The company does not cut useful costs to reach a short-term target.
- **A better reputation** with the customers, the employees and the investors.

**For the economy**

- The capital goes to its **most productive uses**.
- The economy gets **sustainable** growth, innovation and jobs.
- There are fewer **bubbles and crises** (see below).

## The share price today and the long-term value

The share price shows the expectations of the investors about the future cash flows. These expectations can be wrong, or too high. For this reason the maximization of the share price today is not the same as the maximization of the long-term value.

The managers can increase the share price in the short term with actions that destroy future value.

- A cut in the investment, e.g. in R&D or in maintenance. The cash flow increases today and decreases in the future.
- The manipulation of the accounting results.
- Promotion (hype) that creates expectations above the real capacity of the company.

The market can also have a wrong price for a long time, as in a bubble. In the long term the share price follows the cash flows. Thus, a price above the value is not a permanent gain for the shareholders.

## The risks of a focus on the accounting profit

The accounting profit (the net income, also called the **earnings**) is not the same as the value. A focus on the accounting profit has four risks.

- **The profit can be managed.** The company can change its accounting policies or defer expenses to a later period (**earnings management**). The cash does not change.
- **The profit ignores the cost of capital.** The net income deducts the interest on the debt, but not the cost of equity. A company can increase its profit and destroy value, if its return is lower than its cost of capital (see the example in [Value Creation](04-value-creation.md)).
- **The profit ignores the cash flow, the risk and the investment.** The free cash flow deducts the investment in fixed capital and in working capital (see [Operating Decisions](01-operating-decisions.md)). The profit does not.
- **The profit gives incentives to cut useful costs.** The managers cut R&D or marketing to "meet the number" of the quarter.

**Example**

A company with a cost of capital of $10\%$ cuts its R&D by $10$ in year 1. The R&D is a part of the fixed costs $F_t$ in the table of [Operating Decisions](01-operating-decisions.md). Ignore the taxes, as in the model of [Companies and Corporate Finance](00-companies-and-corporate-finance.md). The profit and the free cash flow $\Pi_1$ of year 1 increase by $10$. Without the R&D, the company loses a cash flow of $3$ each year, so each $\Pi_t$ from year 2 forever decreases by $3$. The formula gives the change in the value.

$$\Delta V = \frac{10}{1.10} - \frac{1}{1.10} \times \frac{3}{10\%} = 9.1 - 27.3 = -18.2$$

The profit increases, but the value decreases by $18.2$. The cut is a project with $NPV < 0$, so the rule of [Investment Decisions](02-investment-decisions.md) rejects it. If the investors do not see the effect of the cut, the share price can increase today. It decreases later, when the lost cash flows become visible.

## Shareholders and other stakeholders

The **stakeholders** are all the groups that have an interest in the company, e.g. the employees, the customers, the suppliers, the community and the environment.

**When the interests are complementary**

In most cases the long-term value for the shareholders and the interests of the other stakeholders go together.

- The company treats its employees and customers well. Thus, the productivity and the loyalty increase.
- The company keeps a high quality of its products.
- The company has stable relations with its suppliers.
- The company protects the community and the environment, and this action protects its reputation.

**When the interests are not complementary**

In some cases the interests are in conflict.

- The company reduces its personnel or closes plants to increase its efficiency.
- The company pollutes to reduce its costs. The cost goes to the community, not to the company (an **externality**).
- The company puts pressure on its suppliers for lower prices.
- The company gives up a part of its return for social causes.

**What to do**

The company gives priority to the long-term value for the shareholders, but within the law and the ethics. When there is a conflict, it looks for the solution that causes the least damage to the other groups. The company must not destroy the base that keeps its value, i.e. its customers and its employees.

## Bubbles and crises

The tulip mania (Netherlands, 1630s), the dot-com bubble (late 1990s) and the financial crisis of 2007–2008 have common characteristics.

- **The prices are far above the fundamental value.**
- **Euphoria and herd behavior.** People buy because they are afraid to miss the increase of the prices. They think that "this time is different".
- **Easy credit and too much leverage.** The debt increases the gains when the prices increase, and the losses when the prices decrease (see the costs of financial distress in [Financing Decisions](03-financing-decisions.md)).
- **Speculation instead of an analysis of the value.** The controls fail, or the incentives are not aligned with the value.
- **A sudden collapse** when the expectations change.

In each case the investors forgot the principles of the value. A business creates value only if it generates cash flows with a return above its cost of capital.

## Does more growth always create value?

No. The growth creates value only if the **ROIC is higher than the WACC**. If the ROIC is lower than the WACC, the growth destroys value, and a faster growth destroys value faster (see the growth formula and the table in [Value Creation](04-value-creation.md)).

The growth can also cause other problems.

- **Cost overruns** in the new projects.
- **Expensive acquisitions.** If the company pays more than the present value of the cash flows of the target, the acquisition has $NPV < 0$ (see [Investment Decisions](02-investment-decisions.md)).
- **A loss of focus** on the main business.

The quality of the growth is important, not only the size.

## The role of the boards and the shareholders

The managers act for the shareholders, but their interests can be different, e.g. when the managers get a bonus on the profit of the quarter. This conflict is the **agency problem**. The board of directors and the shareholders can keep the managers focused on the long-term value.

- **Align the compensation of the executives with the long-term value**, e.g. with shares that the executives cannot sell for some years, or with targets on the ROIC and the cash flow.
- **Evaluate the managers with measures of value**, not only with the profit of the quarter.
- **Choose independent directors** who know the business.
- **Communicate openly** with the investors, and limit the quarterly earnings guidance.
- **Be active, long-term shareholders**, with the vote and the dialogue with the managers.

> [!NOTE]
> A target on the ROIC alone can also give wrong incentives. A manager with a ROIC of $20\%$ can refuse a project with a ROIC of $15\%$, because the project reduces the average ROIC. If the WACC is $10\%$, the project creates value. A target on the economic value added (EVA), $(ROIC - WACC) \times IC$, does not have this problem (see [Value Creation](04-value-creation.md)).
