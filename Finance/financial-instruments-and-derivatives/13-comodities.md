# Commodities

## What are commodities?

**Commodities** are physical raw materials that trade on organized markets. Their two defining features are fungibility and standardization.

- **Fungibility** means that one unit is interchangeable with another unit of the same grade.
- **Standardization** means that the quality and the specifications of each unit are uniform.
- **Liquidity** means that the commodity is easy to buy and sell.
- **Volatility** means that prices are sensitive to fundamental factors, such as supply and demand.

**Example**

A barrel of WTI (West Texas Intermediate) crude oil has the same characteristics, whoever the producer is. The NYMEX light sweet crude oil futures contract sets the quality limits (for example a sulfur content of 0.42% or less by weight) and the delivery point at Cushing, Oklahoma. Crude oil from different fields and producers that meets these limits is deliverable, so any barrel can replace another one.

## Classification of commodities

Commodities fall into three main groups.

**Energy**

- Crude oil (WTI, Brent).
- Natural gas.
- Gasoline.
- Heating oil.
- Coal.

**Metals**

- **Precious metals** are gold, silver, platinum, and palladium.
- **Industrial (base) metals** are copper, aluminum, and zinc.

**Agricultural**

- **Grains** are wheat, corn, and soybeans.
- **Soft commodities** are crops that are grown rather than mined, mostly tropical, such as coffee, sugar, cocoa, and cotton. In a wider sense the term also covers grains and livestock.
- **Livestock** is mainly cattle.

## Global economic importance

- **Market size.** Common estimates put global commodity trading at more than 20 trillion dollars per year. The figure depends on the measure, because the notional value of commodity derivatives is much larger than the value of physical trade.
- **Inflation.** Energy and food are key components of the consumer price index (CPI), so commodity prices pass quickly into inflation.
- **Geopolitics.** The control of strategic resources, such as oil, gas, and critical metals, gives countries political power.
- **Economic development.** Many countries depend on the export of raw materials. A country is commodity-dependent when commodities are more than 60% of its merchandise exports, which is true for about two thirds of developing economies.

**Commodity supercycles**

A supercycle is a long period, often 10 to 20 years, of high prices that structural changes in global demand cause. The main example is the industrialization of China in 2000–2010, which increased the demand for metals and energy.

**Key data**

- Oil is about 3% of world GDP. This share changes with the price of oil, from less than 2% in the 1960s to about 7% at the peaks of 1980 and 2008.
- China consumes more than half of the refined copper of the world (about 58% in 2025).

## Spot vs. futures markets

| | Spot market | Futures market |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delivery | Immediate | On an agreed future date |
| Price | Current market price | Price agreed today for future delivery |
| Contract terms | Negotiated between the parties | Standardized by the exchange |
| Settlement | Physical | Physical or cash, and most positions close before delivery |
| Margin | Not required | Required |
| Liquidity | Lower | High |
| Maturity | None | Fixed expiry date |
| Main advantages | The real exchange price of the asset, with no expiry date | Risk hedging, and the timing of purchases and sales to match the needs of inventory |

**Spot-futures relationship**

The futures price of a commodity reflects the spot price today plus the costs to carry the commodity to delivery, which are the storage cost and the funding cost. For a consumption commodity, the convenience yield $y$ reduces the futures price.

$$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r + u - y)T}$$

where $r$ is the funding rate, $u$ is the storage cost as a fraction of the price per annum, and $T$ is the time to delivery (see [Futures and Forwards Pricing](05-futures-and-forwards-pricing.md)). The futures price is not the expected spot price plus these costs. It differs from the expected spot price $E(S_T)$ by a risk premium.

## Main global exchanges

- **CME Group (Chicago)** operates four exchanges, which are CME, CBOT, NYMEX, and COMEX.
  - WTI crude oil and natural gas (NYMEX).
  - Agricultural commodities such as corn, soybeans, and wheat (CBOT).
  - Livestock and meat, such as live cattle (CME).
- **ICE (Intercontinental Exchange)**
  - Brent crude oil and refined products such as gasoil (ICE Futures Europe).
  - Coffee, sugar, cocoa, and cotton (ICE Futures US).
- **LME (London Metal Exchange)**
  - Industrial metals such as copper, aluminum, and zinc.
  - The LME is known for physical delivery through a global network of approved warehouses, with daily prompt dates up to 3 months. Other exchanges also deliver physically (NYMEX WTI, COMEX metals), and on the LME too fewer than 5% of trades end in delivery.
- **COMEX (a division of CME Group)**
  - Precious metals such as gold and silver, and also copper, which is an industrial metal. Platinum and palladium trade on NYMEX.

**Trading hours**

Commodity markets trade almost 24 hours a day, because the assets are global. For example, CME Globex trades energy and metal futures from Sunday to Friday, 6:00 p.m. to 5:00 p.m. New York time, with a daily break of one hour.

## Oil, WTI vs. Brent

| | WTI (West Texas Intermediate) | Brent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quality | Light and sweet, about 39.6° API and 0.24% sulfur | Light and sweet, about 38° API and 0.37% sulfur |
| Delivery | Physical, at Cushing, Oklahoma (inland) | North Sea cargoes, waterborne. The ICE Brent futures settle in cash against the ICE Brent Index |
| Benchmark | For North America | Global, for about two thirds of the internationally traded crude oil |
| Price | Usually lower | Usually higher, because of transport |
| Specific factors | Inventories at Cushing, US shale oil production | Middle East geopolitics, OPEC decisions |

Dated Brent, the physical price, comes from a basket of North Sea grades (Brent, Forties, Oseberg, Ekofisk, Troll), and since 2023 also from cargoes of US WTI Midland delivered to Rotterdam.

**Brent-WTI spread**

Brent is usually 2 to 5 dollars per barrel more expensive than WTI, if there are no bottlenecks. WTI is a little lighter and sweeter, but it is landlocked at Cushing, so it must pay transport by pipeline to the coast to reach world markets.

- **Before 2011**, WTI traded close to Brent, often at a small premium because of its higher quality.
- **From 2011 to 2014**, US shale production filled Cushing faster than pipelines could move the oil out, and the ban on US crude exports kept the surplus inland. The spread reached about 27 to 28 dollars in September 2011.
- **After December 2015**, the US lifted the export ban and new pipelines opened, so the spread narrowed to a few dollars.

## Natural gas and refined products

### Natural gas

**Unique characteristics**

- **Regional markets.** Transport is expensive, by pipeline or as liquefied natural gas (LNG), which needs liquefaction, special ships, and regasification. Thus prices differ from region to region, and LNG trade links the regions more and more.
- **Regional benchmarks** are Henry Hub (United States), NBP (United Kingdom), and TTF (Europe), which the table below compares.
- **Extreme seasonal volatility.** Demand rises in winter for heating and in summer for cooling, through the gas that power plants burn for air conditioning.
- **Limited storage.** Gas is stored underground (depleted fields, salt caverns, aquifers), with a limited capacity. In the United States, storage fills in the injection season (April to October) and empties in the withdrawal season (November to March).

| Benchmark | Region | Type | Quote |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Henry Hub | United States | Physical pipeline hub in Erath, Louisiana, and delivery point of the NYMEX futures (10,000 MMBtu) | USD per MMBtu |
| NBP (National Balancing Point) | United Kingdom | Virtual trading point of the UK grid | Pence per therm |
| TTF (Title Transfer Facility) | Netherlands, benchmark for Europe | Virtual trading point of the Dutch grid | EUR per MWh |

**Main drivers**

- **Weather**, which sets the demand for heating and cooling.
- **Power generation**, because gas-fired plants compete with coal and renewables.
- **Associated production with oil.** Oil wells, for example in the Permian Basin, also produce gas. This supply follows the economics of oil drilling and not the price of gas.

## Gold as a store of value

**Characteristics of gold**

- **Store of value.** Gold protects against inflation over long periods. In the short run it is volatile and can move far above or below its long-run value, so it is a poor inflation hedge over months or a few years.
- **Safe haven.** Investors buy gold in crises. In acute liquidity crises, gold can also fall at first, because investors sell it to raise cash, as in 2008 and March 2020.
- **Negative correlation** with the USD, and with stocks in periods of crisis.
- **No yield.** Gold pays no interest or dividends. A holder can only lend it at the gold lease rate, which is usually small (see [Futures and Forwards Pricing](05-futures-and-forwards-pricing.md)).

**Drivers of the price of gold**

- **Inflation**, which reduces the purchasing power of money and makes a real asset more attractive.
- **Real interest rates.** Gold pays no yield, so the real rate is the opportunity cost of holding it, and the price usually falls when real rates rise. This inverse relationship weakened from 2022, when gold held its value while real rates rose sharply.
- **Strength of the USD.** Gold is priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar makes gold more expensive in other currencies and lowers the dollar price.
- **Geopolitical uncertainty**, which increases the demand for a safe haven.
- **Central bank purchases.** Central banks bought more than 1,000 tonnes per year in 2022 and 2023, the highest levels in the data since 1950, to diversify their reserves away from the dollar.

## Copper as an economic indicator

Copper is often considered the best market indicator of global economic activity, which gives it the name "Dr. Copper". The reason is its wide use in many sectors.

- **Building construction**, with wiring and plumbing, is the largest end use in the United States (about 44%). Worldwide it comes second after equipment.
- **Infrastructure**, such as power cables and pipes.
- **Transport**, such as cars and ships.
- **Electronics**, because of its high electrical conductivity.

Copper is an informal signal, not an official indicator. It moves with industrial activity and sometimes leads it by a few months, but it does not predict every recession, and supply shocks and Chinese demand also move its price.

**Supply factors**

- **Chile** produces about 24% of world mine output (2024), down from about 30% a decade before.
- **The Democratic Republic of the Congo** (about 14%) passed **Peru** (about 12%) in 2024 as the second-largest producer.
- **Declining ore grades** at old mines, so each tonne of ore gives less copper and output falls unless the mines process more ore.
- **CapEx in new projects**, which takes many years from discovery to production.

**Demand factors**

- **China** consumes more than half of the refined copper of the world (about 58% in 2025).
- **The energy transition**, because power grids need large quantities of copper.
- **Electric vehicles**, which use several times as much copper as a car with a combustion engine.
- **Renewable energy**, because wind and solar plants use more copper per unit of power than fossil fuel plants.

## Futures curve and market structures

The futures curve shows the futures prices of one commodity for each delivery date. Its shape follows from the cost-of-carry formula.

$$F_0 = S_0 e^{(r + u - y)T}$$

where $F_0$ is the futures price, $S_0$ is the spot price, $r$ is the risk-free rate, $u$ is the storage cost, and $y$ is the convenience yield, all per annum with continuous compounding. The storage cost is $u$ here, because $c$ is the full cost of carry in [Futures and Forwards Pricing](05-futures-and-forwards-pricing.md).

| | Contango | Backwardation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prices | Futures above spot, $F_0 > S_0$ | Futures below spot, $F_0 < S_0$ |
| Curve | Upward | Downward |
| Condition | $r + u > y$, so the costs of storage and funding are larger than the convenience yield | $y > r + u$, so the convenience yield is larger than the costs of storage and funding |
| Market | Abundant current supply and high inventories | Scarce inventories |

The convenience yield is high when

- inventories are low,
- supply is disrupted,
- seasonal demand is at its peak.

When inventories rise, the convenience yield falls toward zero, so a market with abundant supply goes into contango. When inventories fall, users pay a premium to hold the physical commodity now, so the market goes into backwardation.

### Roll yield and the P&L of the roll

A futures contract expires, so an investor who wants a constant exposure rolls the position, which means to sell the expiring contract and buy the next one before the first notice day or the last trading day (see the section on physical delivery). Commodity indexes roll each month, for example the S&P GSCI moves 20% of the position each day from the 5th to the 9th business day.

The return of a fully collateralized long futures position, where the investor holds the full notional value in Treasury bills, has three parts.

$$R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{spot}} + R_{\text{roll}} + R_{\text{collateral}} \qquad R_{\text{excess}} = R_{\text{spot}} + R_{\text{roll}}$$

- **Spot return.** The change in the price of the front contract, which converges to the spot price at expiry.
- **Roll return (roll yield).** The futures return minus the spot return, which comes from the slope of the curve.
- **Collateral return.** The interest on the Treasury bills, which the excess return leaves out.

**P&L of one monthly roll**

On the roll date $t$, the investor sells the front contract at $F_1(t)$ and buys the second contract at $F_2(t)$. At the next roll date, the second contract is the front contract, at $F_1(t+1)$. The excess return of the month is

$$1 + R_{\text{excess}} = \frac{F_1(t+1)}{F_2(t)} = \underbrace{\frac{F_1(t+1)}{F_1(t)}}_{1 + R_{\text{spot}}} \times \underbrace{\frac{F_1(t)}{F_2(t)}}_{1 + R_{\text{roll}}}$$

The roll return $(F_1 - F_2)/F_2$ is the return of the new contract if the front price does not change, and it is known on the roll date, while the spot return is not. It is positive in backwardation ($F_2 < F_1$), because each new contract is cheaper than the expiring one, and negative in contango ($F_2 > F_1$). With continuous compounding, $12 \ln(F_1/F_2)$ is the annual roll yield of the examples below, with the front contract in place of the spot price.

The roll trade itself has no P&L and does not pay or receive $F_1 - F_2$, because both contracts trade at market prices and a new futures position has a value of zero. The roll yield accrues in the daily settlement while the new contract converges to the front price. The curve shape changes only the number of contracts that the same notional value buys, $N_2 = N_1 F_1 / F_2$, so the investor holds fewer contracts after each roll in contango and more in backwardation.

The roll yield is a gain only if the spot price does not follow the curve. If the futures price equals the expected spot price, a backwardated curve predicts a fall of the spot price that cancels the roll yield (see [Futures and Forwards Pricing](05-futures-and-forwards-pricing.md) for normal backwardation and contango). Historically, the spot prices of commodities in backwardation fell less than their curves predicted, so long futures earned a part of the roll yield as a risk premium that hedgers pay.

### Futures curve trading

Curve trades take positions on the shape of the futures curve, not only on its level.

- **Calendar spreads.** A long position in one maturity and a short position in another maturity of the same commodity, which gains from a change in the slope (see the section on calendar spreads).
- **Carry strategy.** Each month, the trader ranks commodities by their carry $C = (S - F)/F$, the return of a futures contract if the spot price does not change. With the cost-of-carry formula, $C \approx (y - r - u)T$, so the carry is the convenience yield minus the costs of funding and storage. Spot prices are often illiquid, so the trader estimates the carry from the two nearest contracts. The cross-sectional version is long the commodities with the highest carry (backwardation) and short those with the lowest (contango). The time-series version is long each commodity with a positive carry and short each with a negative carry. Long-short carry strategies had an average annualized Sharpe ratio of about 0.8 across asset classes.
- **Deferred contracts.** In many commodities, contango is steepest at the front of the curve, so a long position in a later maturity (for example 6 months) loses less to the roll than a front-month position. A long deferred contract and a short front contract earn the difference between their roll returns.
- **Optimized roll.** Some indexes, such as the S&P GSCI Dynamic Roll and the Bloomberg Roll Select Commodity Index, roll into the contract with the most backwardation or the least contango, not into the next contract.
- **Roll timing.** The roll dates of the large indexes are public, so other traders sell the expiring contract and buy the next one before the index funds, and reverse the trade after. This front-running made the roll more expensive for index investors, by about 3.6% of return per year from 2000 to 2010. Some funds roll earlier or over more days to lower this cost, for example the oil ETF USO moved to a 10-trading-day roll in May 2020.

## Physical delivery vs. cash settlement

| | Physical delivery | Cash settlement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical commodities | Crude oil (NYMEX WTI), LME metals, COMEX metals, some grains (CBOT) | Brent (ICE), energy indexes, lean hogs and feeder cattle (CME), weather derivatives |
| Process | The short side gives notice of delivery to the clearing house before expiry, and the long side receives the commodity | At expiry the exchange settles the open positions in cash against a reference price |
| Specifications or benchmark | Strict quality, location, and certification of the commodity (approved brands and warehouses) | A price assessment of a price reporting agency (Platts, Argus) or an index that the exchange calculates (ICE Brent Index, CME Lean Hog Index) |
| Risk or advantage | Logistic costs, storage, and transport | No logistic risk, so more participants can trade |

**Process of physical delivery**

The notice dates depend on the contract (see [Futures and Forwards](04-futures-and-forwards.md) for the key dates and the three-day delivery process).

- On CBOT grains, the first notice day is the last business day before the delivery month. From then on, the clearing house can assign a delivery to any long position.
- On NYMEX WTI, trading ends three business days before the 25th calendar day of the month before the delivery month. The oil then flows ratably into Cushing during the delivery month.

**Strategy**

Most traders, such as speculators and index funds, do not want the commodity. They close or roll their positions before the first notice day, or before the last trading day for contracts like WTI. A long who keeps the position must take delivery and find storage, as in April 2020 for WTI.

**Cash-settled weather derivatives**

Weather futures settle against an index of temperature, such as heating degree days (HDD) for winter and cooling degree days (CDD) for summer. Nothing can be delivered, so cash settlement is the only possibility.

## Calendar spreads and crack spreads

### Calendar spreads

A calendar spread is a long position in one maturity and a short position in another maturity of the same commodity. The trader bets on the shape of the futures curve, not on its level, so the value of the position is the change in the spread $F_{\text{near}} - F_{\text{far}}$ between the two contracts.

- **Bull calendar spread.** Long the near month and short the far month. It gains when the near contract rises relative to the far one, so when the market moves toward backwardation (stronger backwardation or weaker contango), for example because inventories fall.
- **Bear calendar spread.** Short the near month and long the far month. It gains when the near contract falls relative to the far one, so when the market moves toward contango (stronger contango or weaker backwardation), for example because inventories rise.
- **Advantages.** A move of the whole curve in parallel changes both legs by the same amount and cancels out, so the position has less directional volatility. Thus exchanges require a lower margin for a spread than for two outright positions.

**Example**

A trader buys the near WTI contract at 80 dollars and sells the far contract at 78 dollars, a spread of +2 (backwardation) on 1,000 barrels each.

| Scenario | Near | Far | Spread | Profit of the bull spread |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Start | 80 | 78 | +2 | |
| Inventories fall | 85 | 80 | +5 | $(5 - 2) \times 1{,}000 = 3{,}000$ dollars |
| Parallel rise of 5 dollars | 85 | 83 | +2 | 0 dollars |
| Inventories rise | 79 | 80 | -1 | $(-1 - 2) \times 1{,}000 = -3{,}000$ dollars |

The profit depends only on the change in the spread, not on the direction of the price of oil.

### Crack spreads (3:2:1)

A crack spread is the difference between the price of refined products and the price of the crude oil that they come from, so it measures the gross margin of a refinery. The 3:2:1 crack spread assumes that 3 barrels of crude oil give 2 barrels of gasoline and 1 barrel of heating oil (distillate), close to the output of a typical US refinery.

$$\text{Crack spread} = \frac{2 \times \text{RBOB} + 1 \times \text{HO}}{3} - \text{WTI}$$

where RBOB is the NYMEX gasoline futures, HO is the NYMEX heating oil futures (now NY Harbor ultra-low sulfur diesel), and WTI is the crude oil futures. RBOB and HO are quoted in dollars per gallon and WTI in dollars per barrel, so the product prices must be multiplied by 42 gallons per barrel first. The result is the margin per barrel of crude.

**Example**

With WTI at 75 dollars per barrel, RBOB at 2.40 dollars per gallon ($2.40 \times 42 = 100.80$ dollars per barrel), and HO at 2.70 dollars per gallon ($2.70 \times 42 = 113.40$ dollars per barrel),

$$\text{Crack spread} = \frac{2 \times 100.80 + 113.40}{3} - 75 = 105 - 75 = 30 \text{ dollars per barrel}$$

**Use in trading**

- **Hedge for refineries.** A refinery buys crude and sells products, so it is naturally long the crack spread. To lock in its margin, it sells the crack spread, which means it sells RBOB and HO futures and buys WTI futures in the ratio 3:2:1.
- **Seasonal trading.** Demand for gasoline peaks in the US driving season (from Memorial Day in late May to Labor Day in early September), so the gasoline crack usually widens in spring and summer. The heating oil crack is usually stronger in winter.
- **Indicator of refining margins.** A wide crack spread signals high profits for refineries, and a narrow one signals weak demand for products or expensive crude.

**Popular spreads**

- **Energy.** WTI-Brent (see the section on WTI and Brent) and gasoline-WTI, a crack spread for gasoline only.
- **Metals.** Gold-silver, usually traded as the gold/silver ratio, the number of ounces of silver that buy one ounce of gold.
- **Grains.** Corn-wheat, two grains that substitute for each other in animal feed, and corn-soybeans, which compete for the same farmland. When soybeans cost more than about 2.5 times the price of corn, farmers tend to plant more soybeans, and below that ratio they tend to plant more corn.

## Volatility of commodities vs. other assets

**Typical annualized volatilities**

| Asset | Typical volatility | Reason | Implied volatility index |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Crude oil | 25–35% | Geopolitics and OPEC decisions | OVX |
| Natural gas | 40–80% | Extreme seasonality and limited storage | |
| Gold | 15–25% | Safe haven, the least volatile commodity | GVZ |
| S&P 500 | 15–20% | Diversified index of companies | VIX |

These ranges are typical levels in calm markets. In crises, volatility can rise far above them.

**Situations that increase volatility**

- **Weather**, for agricultural commodities (droughts, floods, frost), and also for natural gas through demand for heating and cooling.
- **Geopolitical tensions**, for energy (wars, sanctions, OPEC decisions).
- **Supply disruptions**, for mining (strikes, accidents, export bans).
- **Changes in the demand of large economies such as China**, for industrial metals.

**Volatility surface**

Many commodities show an upside skew, so out-of-the-money calls have a higher implied volatility than out-of-the-money puts. The reason is that the large price moves come from supply shocks, which make prices spike up, so users buy calls to protect themselves. Equities show the opposite, a downside skew, because crashes are the large moves and investors buy puts to protect their portfolios.

The skew of a commodity changes with the market regime. Crude oil usually has an upside skew when the market fears a supply disruption, but in 2020, with the collapse of demand, its skew pointed to the downside.

## Structure of a commodity trading desk

The typical roles in the commodity desk of an investment bank are flow trading, volatility trading, and structuring.

**Flow trading** (energy, metals, agricultural products)

- **Quoting and market making.** The trader quotes bid and offer prices to clients in futures, swaps, and forwards, and earns the bid-offer spread.
- **Active risk taking.** The trader keeps part of the risk of the client flows when the desk has a view on the market. In US banks, the Volcker Rule bans proprietary trading, so this risk must come from market making for clients.
- **Inventory management and hedging.** The trader hedges the net position of the book with futures and manages any physical inventory, with its storage and financing costs.

**Volatility trading**

- The desk trades options on commodities, with the same tools as in other asset classes (implied volatility, skew, and the Greeks).
- Liquidity is best in energy (crude oil options on WTI and Brent) and in gold.

**Structuring**

- **Hedges for clients with exotic derivatives.** For example, an airline buys fuel every day, so it hedges with Asian (average-price) swaps and options, whose payoff depends on the average price over a month and not on the price on one date.
- **Cash flow matching and solutions.** The desk designs the payments of a hedge to match the cash flows of the client, such as the delivery schedule of a producer or the purchases of a consumer.
- **Commodity indexes and hybrids.** The desk sells products on commodity indexes, such as the S&P GSCI and the Bloomberg Commodity Index, and hybrid products that combine commodities with other assets, such as currencies or interest rates.

## Operational aspects, settlement and logistics

### Daily operations

**Mark-to-market**

- **Settlement prices.** Each day the exchange sets a settlement price, which is the volume-weighted average price of the trades in a short settlement window. For NYMEX WTI, the window is 14:28 to 14:30 New York time (13:30 Chicago time). For ICE Brent, it is 19:28 to 19:30 London time. Each market has its own window, so the settlement time depends on the product.
- **Curve construction for illiquid maturities.** Only the near contracts trade actively. The desk builds the prices of the far maturities from the liquid contracts, from spreads, and from swap quotes, with interpolation.
- **Calibration of volatility surfaces.** The desk fits a model of implied volatility to the option prices that trade, by strike and maturity, and uses it to value the options that do not trade.

**Margin**

- **Initial margin vs. maintenance margin.** The trader deposits the initial margin to open a position. If the balance falls below the maintenance margin, the trader receives a margin call and must restore the balance to the initial margin (see [Futures and Forwards](04-futures-and-forwards.md)).
- **Cross-margining between related products.** Positions in related products partly offset each other, such as WTI against Brent, or crude oil against gasoline and heating oil in a crack spread. Thus the clearing house asks for less margin on the combined position than on each position alone.

### Key deadlines

- **WTI.** Trading ends three business days before the 25th calendar day of the month before the delivery month. The notices of delivery follow after the last trading day, so a long position must close before that day to avoid delivery.
- **Brent.** Since the March 2016 contract, ICE Brent futures expire on the last business day of the second month before the delivery month, and settle in cash against the ICE Brent Index. Before that, they expired 15 days before the delivery month.
- **LME.** Daily prompt dates up to 3 months forward give a rolling window of delivery dates, so a position can settle on almost any business day and not only on one date each month.
