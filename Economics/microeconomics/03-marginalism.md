# Marginalism

> Individuals use the first unit of a good to meet the most important and pressing needs related to it. They will use the second unit to meet the second most pressing need. As the quantity of the good they own increases, the needs that are met are less valuable and less pressing.
>
> — Saifedean Ammous, *Principles of Economics* (2023), ch. 2, p. 37

In daily life, we make marginal decisions. We do not value goods in absolute terms, as "all or nothing". We decide if we want one more unit of a good, or one unit less. Which is more valuable, water or diamonds? In this general form, the question has no economic answer, because nobody has to choose between all the water and all the diamonds in the world. Valuations are made at the margin, and the economic way of thinking replaces the "all or nothing" view with the analysis of the marginal benefits and costs of each choice.

## The Marginal Revolution

In 1871, Carl Menger published his *Principles of Economics*, and he started the marginal analysis of economic questions. In the same years, William Stanley Jevons (1871) and Léon Walras (1874) found similar ideas independently, so historians call this period the "marginal revolution". Ammous explains that the new method gave a different alternative to the older ways to study society, which looked at history through the will of God, nature, the nation, race, or the church. Marginal analysis showed that we understand society better when we study its main driving forces: the choices and actions of individuals.

The Austrian school of economics grew around Menger in Vienna. Walras put his own version of marginalism inside a model of general equilibrium. This model, with its mathematics and its relations between aggregates, became the dominant tradition in modern economics. The Austrian tradition continued to study the choices of individuals, without the use of aggregates.

## Marginal Utility

Menger first showed that value is not a property of goods. Value is subjective, because it depends on the ability of a good to satisfy our needs. Menger then applied this idea to the different units of the same good. Different satisfactions have unequal importance for us, so the different units of the same good also have unequal value. Each unit gets its value from the need that it satisfies. Thus, the same good can have a different value for the same person at different times, according to the need that it meets.

The marginal unit is the unit that an individual adds or removes in a decision. Marginal utility is the importance that he gives to that unit. Mises defines utility as the importance that we attach to a thing, because we believe that it can remove our uneasiness. In the Austrian view, utility is ranked and not measured. An individual can say that he prefers one satisfaction to another, but he cannot say by how many units he prefers it.

## Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility

An individual uses the first unit of a good for his most urgent need, and each additional unit for a less urgent need. As the quantity of the good increases, the needs that he satisfies become less important. Thus, each marginal unit has less value for him than the unit before it. This is the law of diminishing marginal utility.

Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk gives a famous example. An isolated farmer has five sacks of grain, and he cannot sell grain or buy more of it. He uses the sacks in this order of importance:

1. The first sack gives him the bread that he needs to stay alive.
2. The second sack gives him more food, so that he is strong and can work.
3. The third sack feeds his poultry, so that his diet includes meat.
4. The fourth sack gives him brandy.
5. The fifth sack feeds his parrots, which amuse him.

Each sack satisfies a less important need than the sack before it. Mises explains that this law does not come from physiology or psychology. It comes from the nature of action itself. An individual always uses a stock of means for his most important ends first, so additional units go to less and less important ends.

## Valuation by the Least Valuable Use

All the sacks of the farmer are the same, so he can use any one of them for any of his needs. If he loses one sack, he does not reduce each use by one fifth. He stops the least important use, and he does not feed the parrots. The loss of any sack costs him only the satisfaction of the least important use. For this reason, each sack has the same value for him, and this value is the satisfaction that the parrots give him.

Menger states this as a general rule. The value of one unit of a stock is equal to the importance of the least important satisfaction that the whole stock provides. Ammous concludes that the value of a good does not depend on its total or abstract utility. It depends on the quantity of the good that we have, in relation to our needs. Humans decide on the basis of the utility of specific quantities of a good, and of the ability of these quantities to satisfy their specific needs.

## Water-Diamond Paradox

Adam Smith described the paradox in *The Wealth of Nations*:

> Nothing is more useful than water: but it will purchase scarce any thing; scarce any thing can be had in exchange for it. A diamond, on the contrary, has scarce any value in use; but a very great quantity of other goods may frequently be had in exchange for it.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), bk. I, ch. IV

The classical economists separated "value in use" from "value in exchange", and they explained the price of a good with the labor or the cost that its production needs. Marginal analysis solves the paradox in a different way. Nobody chooses between all the water and all the diamonds. An individual chooses between one glass of water and one diamond. Water is abundant for most people, so the marginal unit of water satisfies a need with low importance, for example to wash a car. Diamonds are scarce, so the marginal diamond satisfies a need with high importance for its owner. Thus, the marginal diamond has more value than the marginal glass of water, although the total stock of water is much more important than the total stock of diamonds.

The answer changes with the quantity that is available. For a traveler who is lost in a desert, with no water and a bag of diamonds, one glass of water can be worth more than all his diamonds. The good is the same, but the margin is different.
