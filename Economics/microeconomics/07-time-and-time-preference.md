# Time and Time Preference

> Man is subject to the passing of time. He comes into existence, grows, becomes old, and passes away. His time is scarce. He must economize it as he economizes other scarce factors. The economization of time has a peculiar character because of the uniqueness and irreversibility of the temporal order.
>
> — Ludwig von Mises, *Human Action* (1949), ch. V, §3

## Time

Time is scarce and subjectively useful, so it is a means. Time has an objective side and a subjective side. The objective side is the time that a clock measures. The subjective side is the value that each person gives to his time. This value depends on his circumstances. One hour has a different value for a student before an exam, for a retired person, and for a patient in a hospital.

Time is different from all other means.

- **It is irreversible.** You cannot buy back the time that you spent on something.
- **It cannot be stored or increased.** You can accumulate goods, but you cannot accumulate hours.
- **Its quantity is uncertain.** Nobody knows how long he will live. A life can last one hour or one hundred years.
- **It is necessary for all actions.** Each action takes time, and each production process takes time.

Mises explains that people must economize time even in a land of plenty. In such a place, nobody needs to economize goods. But a mortal person still cannot enjoy all satisfactions at the same moment, so he must still decide how to use his time.

### The Ultimate Resource

In *The Ultimate Resource* (1981), Julian Simon argues that the ultimate resource is people, with their skills and their imagination. Saifedean Ammous builds on this argument. For him, the ultimate resource is human time, because people can use their time to produce any economic good.

The word "resource" suggests a fixed stock that humans use up. But raw materials, metals, and fuels are not given to us. People must find them, extract them, and transform them, and this work takes time. The quantity of most materials on Earth is far greater than our ability to use them. Thus, the quantity of a material does not make it scarce. The time that people need to produce it makes it scarce. For Ammous, economic scarcity is ultimately the scarcity of human time.

Two trends support this argument. Over time, people produce more material goods, so the price of these goods falls in terms of hours of labor. But the time of each person does not increase, so the value of an hour of labor rises. A famous bet shows the first trend. In 1980, Simon bet the biologist Paul Ehrlich that the prices of five metals would fall in the next ten years, and Ehrlich expected them to rise. In 1990, the inflation-adjusted prices of all five metals were lower, and Ehrlich paid Simon.

## Time Preference

Time preference is the preference for an earlier satisfaction over a later satisfaction. Mises states it as a law:

> Satisfaction of a want in the nearer future is, other things being equal, preferred to that in the farther distant future. Present goods are more valuable than future goods.
>
> — Ludwig von Mises, *Human Action* (1949), ch. XVIII, §2

Ammous gives two reasons for this preference. First, life is uncertain, so a person who waits may never enjoy the good. Second, a durable good gives services for a longer period if the person gets it earlier. Mises gives a more basic reason. A person who did not prefer the present would never consume. He would always save for a later date, and he would never act to satisfy a want.

Time preference is always positive, but its degree changes from person to person and over time.

- **High time preference.** The person wants to satisfy his wants now, and he gives little value to the future. He spends his income instead of saving it, or he works today instead of studying for a better job.
- **Low time preference.** The person gives up present satisfaction to get more in the future. He saves, invests, studies, or plants a tree that will give fruit only after many years.

The interest rate is the market expression of time preference. A lender gives up a present good, so he asks for more future goods in return. When people in a society have a lower time preference, they save more, and interest rates tend to fall.

## Economizing Time

Each person wants to spend his time in leisure, which is the time that he spends on activities that he enjoys for their own sake. But a life of only leisure ends in hunger and an early death. People also want to live long and to improve their future. Thus, each person must decide how much present leisure to give up for future benefits.

For Ammous, the most important trade of each person is the trade with his future self. The simplest example is labor. A person stops his leisure and works to get food and shelter. Work is unpleasant now, but it gives rewards later. Reason lets people imagine a better future and the consequences of a failure to provide for it. Thus, reason lowers time preference. It leads people to work, save, and accumulate durable goods and capital.

This process reinforces itself. Saving and capital increase productivity, so each hour of work produces more. The future becomes less uncertain, so people value it more and save even more. They also tend to live longer, so they have more time. Hans-Hermann Hoppe describes this process:

> Once it is low enough to allow for any savings and capital or durable consumer-goods formation at all, a tendency toward a fall in the rate of time preference is set in motion, accompanied by a "process of civilization."
>
> — Hans-Hermann Hoppe, *Democracy: The God That Failed* (2001), ch. 1
