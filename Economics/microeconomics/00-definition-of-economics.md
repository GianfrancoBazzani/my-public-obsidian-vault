# Definition of Economics

> Economics is not about things and tangible material objects; it is about men, their meanings and actions. Goods, commodities, and wealth and all the other notions of conduct are not elements of nature; they are elements of human meaning and conduct. He who wants to deal with them must not look at the external world; he must search for them in the meaning of acting men.
>
> — Ludwig von Mises, *Human Action* (1949), ch. IV, §1

## What Is Economics

**Economics** is the social science that studies the processes of human interaction that have the purpose to create wealth.

Do not confuse economics with **the economy**. Economics is the science. The economy is the system of production and trade in a country or a region.

- **Social science.** Economics studies people, not physical objects. Thus, it is not a natural science like physics.
- **Processes of human interaction.** Economics studies how people act together. For example, people buy, sell, trade, lend, and make agreements.
- **To create wealth.** People act to get more value. In this definition, wealth is not only money or goods. Wealth is anything that people value and that helps them get their goals.

## Microeconomics vs Macroeconomics

Economics has two main parts: microeconomics and macroeconomics. The names come from Greek: *mikros* means small, and *makros* means large. Different schools of economics separate the two parts with different criteria, so their definitions are also different.

### The Mainstream View: the Scale of the Study

Most textbooks, such as those of N. Gregory Mankiw and Olivier Blanchard, separate the two parts by the scale of the study. Microeconomics studies one agent or one market. Macroeconomics studies a country or the world.

Macroeconomics uses aggregates. An aggregate is one number that adds the values of many agents. For example, the GDP adds the value of all the final goods and services that a country produces in one year.

| | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of study | One person, one company, one market | A country or the world |
| Main variables | Price of one good, quantity, cost, profit | GDP, inflation, unemployment, interest rates |
| Example | The price of bread in a city | The general level of prices in a country |

### The Austrian View: Human Action and Public Policies

The Austrian school sees macroeconomics in a different way, for three reasons:

1. **Only individuals act.** The Austrian school uses methodological individualism. A GDP or a general level of prices does not act or decide. Friedrich Hayek criticized these aggregates in *Prices and Production* (1931) and in his Nobel lecture, "The Pretence of Knowledge" (1974). Thus, a macroeconomic explanation must start from individual actions.
2. **The free market coordinates itself.** Prices and entrepreneurs coordinate the plans of many people. Thus, a general crisis needs a cause that affects all the economy at the same time. Murray Rothbard stated this problem in *America's Great Depression* (1963, ch. 1): "why is there a sudden general cluster of business errors?"
3. **State intervention in money and credit is the main cause of general crises.** In the Austrian business cycle theory, the cause of the cycle is the credit expansion by the central bank and the banks. This new credit does not come from real savings, and the state permits and increases it. Ludwig von Mises gave the first statement of this theory in *The Theory of Money and Credit* (1912). Hayek developed it in *Monetary Theory and the Trade Cycle* (1929) and *Prices and Production* (1931). Jesús Huerta de Soto developed it further in *Money, Bank Credit, and Economic Cycles* (1998).

For these reasons, this note summarizes the Austrian view in one question: "What happens to the economy when the state changes the money, the credit, the taxes, or the public spending?" From this question, this note uses a definition of macroeconomics based on public policies:

**Macroeconomics** is the study of the response of the economic variables to public policies.

This definition is a summary of this note, not a quote from an Austrian author. Not all Austrian economists accept macroeconomics as a separate field:

- **Mises rejected the macroeconomic approach.** In *The Ultimate Foundation of Economic Science* (1962, ch. 5, §6), he wrote that the authors who use a "macroeconomic approach" in place of the analysis of individuals "delude themselves and their public." He also called the national income "a mere political slogan devoid of any cognitive value."
- **Roger Garrison developed an Austrian macroeconomics** in *Time and Money: The Macroeconomics of Capital Structure* (2001). For Garrison, the main subject is intertemporal coordination, the match between the structure of production and the time preferences of people. Policy is the usual cause of discoordination. For example, a central bank can keep the interest rate below its natural level.

The two views overlap in practice. Mainstream macroeconomists also study many policy responses. For example, they study the effect on inflation when the central bank increases the interest rates.

The policy definition has one limit. Microeconomics also studies responses to policies, such as a rent cap, a minimum wage, or a tax on one good. Thus, the definition needs one implicit condition: the policies affect the economy as a whole, such as monetary policy and fiscal policy.

### A Common Confusion: the Definition of Robbins

Lionel Robbins gave a famous definition in *An Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science* (1932, p. 15):

> Economics is the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses.

This is a definition of economics as a whole, not of macroeconomics. It is applicable to both parts, so it cannot separate them.

The Austrian school also criticizes this definition. Robbins supposes that the agent knows the ends and the means, and only calculates the best allocation. For the Austrian school, people also discover new ends and new means. Israel Kirzner explained this difference in *Competition and Entrepreneurship* (1973). For this reason, this note defines economics as the study of processes of human interaction, and not as the allocation of scarce resources.

## Why Economics Is Important

An understanding of economics is fundamental to navigate our world. Each day, we make decisions that have economic effects. Some examples of these questions:

- Is it a good time to open a real estate company?
- Is it a good time to buy a house with a variable-rate mortgage?
- Is it better for me to study or to work?
- Should I keep my company open, or is it better to close it and open a business in a different sector?
- Should I invest my savings in a house to rent it out, if the law sets maximum prices on rent?

Economics does not give one correct answer to each question. It gives us the tools to think about each question correctly.
