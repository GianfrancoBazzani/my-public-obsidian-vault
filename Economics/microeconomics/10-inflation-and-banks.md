# Inflation

Inflation is a general and continuous increase of prices. In other words, each unit of money buys fewer goods and services over time. Inflation is probably one of the worst phenomena in an economy. It is not only that prices are higher, and that individuals must pay more, in relation to their income, for the same goods and services. Entrepreneurs also become uncertain about their future costs, so they reduce or stop their investments and their production. Prices tell each individual what others value and which uses of resources are profitable. When all prices change continuously, these signals become less clear, and the errors of economic calculation increase.

Austrian economists use a narrower definition. Mises uses the word inflation for an increase in the quantity of money, and he calls the general increase of prices a consequence of inflation. This note uses the common definition, but the next section shows that the two definitions are closely related.

## Causes

- **Non-monetary causes (uncommon).** A reduction in the supply of some goods, for example after a war, a bad harvest, or an oil crisis, increases their prices. If the quantity of money stays the same, people who spend more on these goods must spend less on other goods. Thus, the prices of other goods tend to fall, and the general increase of prices is limited and temporary.
- **Monetary causes (very common).** The supply of money increases more than the demand for money. When there is more money for the same quantity of goods, each unit of money loses purchasing power, and prices increase.

Milton Friedman summarizes the second cause in a famous sentence:

> Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon in the sense that it is and can be produced only by a more rapid increase in the quantity of money than in output.
>
> — Milton Friedman, *The Counter-Revolution in Monetary Theory* (1970)

## Governments

The monetary cause of inflation is directly related to governments, through their fiscal policy, and to central banks, through their monetary policy. When a government spends more than it collects in taxes, it must borrow the difference. If the central bank buys this debt with new money, the quantity of money increases, and inflation follows.

Inflation continues because the incentives of governments are not aligned with its elimination. Inflation is a hidden tax. It transfers purchasing power from the holders of money to the issuer of the new money, and no law or vote approves this transfer. It also reduces the real value of public debt. The government repays the same nominal amount, but this amount buys less than when the government borrowed it. Thus, the holders of government bonds lose, and the government dilutes its debt.

The Spanish Jesuit Juan de Mariana described this problem in 1609, in his *Treatise on the Alteration of Money*. To pay for its wars, the crown of Spain reduced the metal content of the copper coins (*vellón*), but kept their legal value. Mariana argued that this debasement was a tax without the consent of the people, because "the private goods of citizens are not at the disposal of the king". He also predicted that the prices of goods would increase in proportion to the loss of weight or quality of the coins.

## Central Banks

Central banks control the quantity of money in the economy. They also set the interest rate at which commercial banks borrow from them, and this rate influences all the other interest rates in the economy. Their influence on the economy is very large:

- If a central bank increases the quantity of money too much, it causes inflation and damages the economy.
- If a central bank restricts the quantity of money too much, it can cause a recession, or at least a reduction of economic growth.

Central banks are not infallible. Nobody, and thus no central bank, knows the correct quantity of money or the correct interest rate for the whole economy. For this reason, the decisions of a central bank can cause undesired economic consequences.
