# Human Action

> Action is will put into operation and transformed into an agency, is aiming at ends and goals, is the ego's meaningful response to stimuli and to the conditions of its environment, is a person's conscious adjustment to the state of the universe that determines his life.
>
> — Ludwig von Mises, *Human Action* (1949), ch. I, §1

Human action is any deliberate behavior or conduct of a human being. The individual chooses an end, selects the means that he thinks will reach it, and acts. The Austrian school starts its analysis from this concept. Mises calls the general science of human action praxeology, and he sees economics as its most developed part. This is why the quote at the start of [Definition of Economics](00-definition-of-economics.md) says that economics "is about men, their meanings and actions".

An economist does not study why a person chooses a specific end. Psychology, history, and other sciences study the motives, the feelings, and the values that come before an action. Economics takes the ends as given, and it studies the logical implications of the fact that people act. Mises writes that "the field of our science is human action, not the psychological events which result in an action." For example, economics does not explain why a person wants a book. It explains what happens to the price of books when more people want them.

## Characteristics

1. **Voluntary.** The individual chooses the action, and he could choose differently. Mises writes that "action is not only doing but no less omitting what possibly could be done." Thus, a person who decides to wait, or to endure a situation that he could change, also acts. Even a threat does not remove the choice. If a robber asks for your money, you still choose between your money and the risk to your life, but the threat removes your better alternatives.
2. **Conscious.** Action is the result of a decision. Mises writes that "conscious or purposeful behavior is in sharp contrast to unconscious behavior, i.e., the reflexes" and the involuntary responses of the body. A sneeze, the beat of the heart, or the contraction of the pupil in strong light are not actions, because nobody decides them.
3. **Habits and customs.** Much of our daily behavior is routine, and we do not deliberate about it each time. A habit is still action, because the person can examine it and change it. Mises writes that "indulgence in a routine which possibly could be changed is action." He gives the example of a person who grows up where the water is clean, and who drinks and washes without care. If this person moves to a place where the water is contaminated, he starts to pay attention to these routines and changes them. Habits and customs save time and attention, because the individual does not need to decide again about each routine matter.
4. **Creativity.** Creativity is the main difference between humans and animals. An animal responds to its environment with instincts. A human imagines future situations that are different from all past situations, and acts to make them real. An entrepreneur who imagines a new product, a new company, or a new use for a resource creates something that past data do not contain. Israel Kirzner describes this as the alertness of the entrepreneur to opportunities that others did not notice. Jesús Huerta de Soto goes further. For him, entrepreneurship is the same as human action, and each action creates new information. For this reason, the future is not a repetition of the past, and economics cannot find constant relations in human behavior (see [Personal Interests and Incentives](01-personal-interests-and-incentives.md)).
5. **Not observable.** We can observe the behavior of a person, but not his ends, his valuations, or the alternatives that he rejected. Two people can do the same physical act for different purposes. One person runs for his health, another runs to catch a bus, and a third runs to escape a danger. Values exist only in the mind of the individual, so an economist cannot measure them. Murray Rothbard calls this idea demonstrated preference. A choice shows that the individual preferred the chosen alternative to the rejected ones, but it does not show by how much. The rejected alternatives are also the origin of the [opportunity cost](02-opportunity-cost.md) of each action.

## Action, Purpose, and Reason

Mises defines human action as "purposeful behavior". The word "purposeful" separates action from reflexes and instinctive responses. Rothbard, a student of Mises, adds the dimension of time:

> Action is purposeful behavior directed toward the attainment of ends in some future period which will involve the fulfillment of wants otherwise remaining unsatisfied.
>
> — Murray Rothbard, *Man, Economy, and State* (1962), ch. 1, §2

Each action takes time, so its end is always in the future. A person acts when he feels uneasy, imagines a better state, and expects that his action can bring it about.

Action requires reason. Humans know that causes produce effects, so they can select the means to reach their ends. They also know that other people have their own ends, so they can cooperate and exchange with them.

## The Primacy of Human Action

Saifedean Ammous gives a useful mental image. Think of the physical world as inert modeling clay. The clay has no shape, value, or purpose of its own. Human reason and human action mold it into goods, and give it value, meaning, and purpose. For example, iron ore in the ground is only a rock until somebody imagines a use for it, extracts it, and transforms it into a tool.

Thus, we understand the material world better when we study it as the product of human reason and action. Explanations of social phenomena through physical objects, abstract nouns, or collective entities fail. The stars do not act, and neither do "the market", "the nation", or "society". Only individuals act. Mises states this principle of methodological individualism directly: "all actions are performed by individuals." If you want to understand the conditions of the material world, study the actions of the people who mold it.

## Rationality

In the Austrian tradition, human action is always rational. Mises writes that "human action is necessarily always rational", and that the term "rational action" is thus pleonastic. The word "rational" does not have its common meaning here:

- It does not mean that the action is correct according to an objective criterion.
- It does not mean that the action is effective, or that it reaches the end of the individual.
- It does not mean that other people approve of the action, morally or otherwise.

"Rational" means only that the action is the product of deliberative reason. When a person reasons and acts, he acts rationally. A person may regret an action and see later that it did not help him reach his ends. This does not change the rationality of the act, because the act was the product of his deliberation, correct or faulty. Other people may judge the action as wrong, but their judgment also does not change its rational nature.

Mises explains that "the opposite of action is not irrational behavior, but a reactive response to stimuli on the part of the bodily organs and instincts which cannot be controlled by the volition of the person concerned." A mistaken action is still rational:

> An action unsuited to the end sought falls short of expectation. It is contrary to purpose, but it is rational, i.e., the outcome of a reasonable—although faulty—deliberation and an attempt—although an ineffectual attempt—to attain a definite goal.
>
> — Ludwig von Mises, *Human Action* (1949), ch. I, §4

This concept is different from rationality in mainstream economics. In the neoclassical model, a rational agent has consistent preferences and chooses the alternative that maximizes his utility. Behavioral economics then finds many choices that break this model, and calls them irrational. For example, the expected monetary value of a lottery ticket is lower than its price, so the purchase is irrational in the neoclassical sense. For Mises, the buyer acts rationally. He deliberately chose the ticket as a means to an end, such as the hope of a large prize or the fun of the game. The economist does not judge the end. He studies the consequences of the choice.
