# Personal Interests and Incentives

## Personal Interest

Why do individuals perform economic actions? Because they pursue their own interests. Mises writes that "the incentive that impels a man to act is always some uneasiness", because a man perfectly content with his state of affairs would have no reason to change it. Uneasiness alone is not sufficient. The individual must also imagine a more satisfactory state of affairs, and he must expect that his action can remove or at least reduce the uneasiness. If one of these three conditions is missing, he does not act. It is important to note that personal interest does not mean selfishness. The ends that an individual chooses can be material or spiritual, and they can include the well-being of his family, his community, or strangers. What matters is that the ends are his own, because only the individual can rank his goals and only he can judge whether an action made him better off.

> It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest. We address ourselves, not to their humanity, but to their self-love, and never talk to them of our own necessities, but of their advantages.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), bk. I, ch. II

## Incentives

Incentives are the rewards and costs that an individual expects from each alternative that he has. When incentives change, the expected outcome of each alternative changes, and so can the action that the individual chooses. Individuals tend to respond to incentives in a predictable direction, but not in a predictable size. Mises explains that we can predict that, other things being equal, a fall in the demand for a good will lower its price, but we cannot predict the extent of this drop, because in the field of human action there are no constant relations. Sometimes the response even goes in the opposite direction. In a study of ten day-care centers in Haifa, Gneezy and Rustichini found that a fine for parents who picked up their children late increased the number of late parents, and the number did not fall when the fine was removed. The parents saw the fine as a price for extra time, not as a punishment.

Love, social recognition, fame, and sex are powerful incentives. Even when someone altruistically helps another person, he acts to reach his own ends, because the well-being of that person is one of his goals. Mises calls action "selfish" only in this formal sense, and he writes that "even an action directly aiming at the improvement of other people's conditions is selfish", because the actor finds it more satisfactory to help than not to help. This does not mean that altruism is a disguise for personal fulfillment or social recognition.

## Cooperation

An individual pursues his own interests, but he cannot reach most of his goals alone. In a society based on the division of labor, he reaches them through voluntary exchange with other people. Nobody is obliged to trade with him, so he must offer something that others value more than what they give in return. To do this, he must do three things.

1. Provide what other individuals want.
2. Provide it with the best possible quality and at the lowest possible price.
3. Make the other party better off with each exchange.

A voluntary exchange benefits both parties, because each party values what he receives more than what he gives. If this were not true, he would not trade. The individuals who best serve the wants of others are the ones who, through social cooperation, reach their own goals. Mises states this for the entrepreneur, who "can succeed only by best serving the consumers", because "his profit depends on the approval of his conduct by the consumers".

Individuals are moved by their own interests, but to satisfy them they must cooperate with others and satisfy the interests of others. Mises writes that people "do not cooperate under the division of labor because they love or should love one another. They cooperate because this best serves their own interests."

## Institutions

Personal interest does not always lead to cooperation. Sometimes the incentives are too weak, and sometimes they are too strong. Incentives are too weak when an individual cannot keep the benefits of his action. Incentives are too strong when an individual can push part of the costs of his action onto other people. Mises calls these two cases external economies and external costs.
 
The rules of a society decide whether personal interests and social interests point in the same direction. North defines institutions as "the rules of the game in a society or, more formally, \[...\] the humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction", and he explains that they structure the incentives in human exchange. Good institutions align personal interests with social interests.

- **Private property.** The owner gets the benefits and bears the costs of the use of his goods, so he has an incentive to use them well and to conserve them. Mises calls private ownership of the means of production "the fundamental institution of the market economy".
- **Freedom of contract.** Individuals are free to agree on the terms of an exchange. An exchange takes place only when both parties expect to benefit from it.
- **Free competition.** Any individual can offer a better or cheaper product. A producer keeps his customers only if he serves them better than his competitors do.
- **Rule of law.** Hayek describes the rule of law as a government that is bound in all its actions by rules fixed and announced in advance. With such rules, each individual can predict how the authorities will use their coercive power, and he can plan his own affairs.
- **Money.** Mises defines money as "the generally accepted and commonly used medium of exchange". Without money, an exchange is possible only when each party wants exactly what the other party offers. With money, each individual can sell what he produces to anyone and buy what he wants from anyone, so the circle of cooperation becomes much larger. Money also makes economic calculation possible, because prices in money show each individual what others value and which uses of resources are profitable. Menger explains that money was not invented by a government, but emerged spontaneously, when individuals in pursuit of their own interests started to accept the most marketable goods in exchange.

History supports this argument. Acemoglu and Robinson compare inclusive institutions, with secure property rights, an impartial system of law, and a level playing field for exchange and contracts, with extractive institutions, which enrich a small elite at the expense of the rest of society. In inclusive institutions, people keep most of the gains from their own efforts, so they have an incentive to work, save, invest, and innovate. In extractive institutions, this incentive is weak, and the economy does not grow.

Human capital and capital goods are necessary for economic growth, but they are not sufficient. A society with skilled workers and modern machines stays poor if its rules let a few people take what others produce. Good institutions are the condition that makes the other factors productive, because they align personal interests with social interests. When individuals can keep the gains of their efforts, they work, save, invest, and innovate, and this brings economic growth, wealth, and social well-being.
