# The Problem of Knowledge

> The peculiar character of the problem of a rational economic order is determined precisely by the fact that the knowledge of the circumstances of which we must make use never exists in concentrated or integrated form but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess.
>
> — Friedrich A. Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945)

The main question of economics is how a society can use its scarce means to reach the most valued ends (see [Ends and Means](02-ends-and-means.md)). Friedrich Hayek shows that this is a problem of knowledge. The knowledge that economic activity needs never exists in one mind or in one institution. It is dispersed in small pieces among all the individuals of a society. These pieces are incomplete, and they often contradict each other.

## Kinds of Knowledge

Hayek distinguishes two kinds of knowledge. The first kind is scientific knowledge, which is knowledge of general rules. A body of experts can collect and use this kind of knowledge. The second kind is "the knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place." This knowledge is not scientific, but Hayek shows that it is very important for the economic problem. He gives examples:

> To know of and put to use a machine not fully employed, or somebody's skill which could be better utilized, or to be aware of a surplus stock which can be drawn upon during an interruption of supplies, is socially quite as useful as the knowledge of better alternative techniques.
>
> — Friedrich A. Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945)

Hayek also mentions the shipper who uses the empty or half-filled journeys of ships, the estate agent who knows temporary opportunities, and the arbitrageur who gains from local differences of prices. Each of them has knowledge that others do not have, and this knowledge is necessary for the correct use of resources.

Randy Barnett uses a different division. He distinguishes two kinds of knowledge, personal knowledge and local knowledge, by their domain of access.

### Personal Knowledge

Barnett defines personal knowledge as the knowledge unique to each person of his perceptions, of his preferences, needs, and desires, of his abilities, and of his opportunities. Nobody else can have it. The content of each person's personal knowledge is different, but all persons have personal knowledge. Each person uses it to value his situation and to allocate scarce means to his own ends.

Access to personal knowledge is limited because most of it is tacit. Barnett follows Michael Polanyi, who writes that "we can know more than we can tell." A person can drive a car or play a musical instrument well, but he cannot explain to others how to do it. Thus, a person cannot give most of his personal knowledge to a planner, even if he wants to.

### Local Knowledge

Barnett defines local knowledge as "that publicly accessible knowledge of resource use, the access to which is limited to certain associations of people." Unlike personal knowledge, local knowledge is public. People can state it and share it with others. The people who attend a meeting, or who take part in a conversation, have local knowledge of it.

The group that has access to local knowledge can be very large. Barnett gives the example of 65,000 people who watch the same football game in a stadium, and millions more who watch it on television. Their knowledge of the game is still local, because the billions of people who do not watch it do not have this knowledge. Knowledge of a language, of concepts, and of a specialty is also local knowledge. Scientific experts and lawyers have access to knowledge that most people do not have, and could not understand.

In principle, anyone can get local knowledge. But access to it is costly, so in practice, each person has access to only a small part of it. Barnett writes that "we are all 'experts' in some domain of local knowledge to which others lack access." Thus, for Barnett, the knowledge of experts that Hayek calls scientific is also local knowledge.

## Knowledge Changes

Knowledge is not static. The circumstances change all the time. Barnett writes that knowledge "is far more like a turbulent stream than a tranquil pool." Hayek concludes that "the economic problem of society is mainly one of rapid adaptation to changes in the particular circumstances of time and place." Thus, the people who know these circumstances directly must make the decisions.

This is why central planning cannot find the best allocation of scarce resources. Even if a person could state all his knowledge, nobody could collect it, combine it, and process it as fast as it changes. A planner never has the data as something "given". But the models of equilibrium that Hayek criticizes start from this assumption. His argument is part of the debate about [economic calculation](07-economic-calculation.md) under socialism. Ludwig von Mises started this debate in 1920, and Hayek continued it against authors such as Oskar Lange.

## The First-Order Problem of Knowledge

In *The Structure of Liberty* (1998), the legal scholar Randy Barnett uses Hayek's ideas to explain why a society needs justice and the rule of law. He calls the first problem of a society the first-order problem of knowledge. This problem has two parts:

1. Each person must be able to act on his personal knowledge, and on the local knowledge that he has as a member of an association.
2. When he acts, he must take into account the knowledge of others, of which he is almost completely ignorant.

Each person lives among other people, and his actions affect their plans. How can each person act on his own knowledge, and also take into account the knowledge of others?

Barnett's answer is that each person needs a domain where he can act on his knowledge without the permission of others. Two rights give this domain:

- **Several property.** Individuals and associations have the right to acquire, possess, use, and dispose of scarce physical resources, including their own bodies. Each owner decides how to use his resources with his personal and local knowledge.
- **Freedom of contract.** The consent of the owner is necessary and sufficient to transfer his rights. Nobody can take his rights without his consent, and he can transfer them to anyone who agrees. Through consensual exchanges, each person uses the knowledge of others, and the prices of these exchanges communicate this knowledge to everyone.

## The Price System

Hayek does not only show that central planning is impossible. He also explains how a society solves the problem of knowledge: with the price system. Prices communicate knowledge, like a system of telecommunications:

> It is more than a metaphor to describe the price system as a kind of machinery for registering change, or a system of telecommunications which enables individual producers to watch merely the movement of a few pointers, as an engineer might watch the hands of a few dials, in order to adjust their activities to changes of which they may never know more than is reflected in the price movement.
>
> — Friedrich A. Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945)

Hayek's example is tin. If tin becomes scarcer, because of a new use or because a source of supply disappears, its price increases. The users of tin use less of it, but they do not need to know the cause. The change continues through the whole economy, to the users of the substitutes of tin and to the users of their substitutes. Hayek writes:

> The marvel is that in a case like that of a scarcity of one raw material, without an order being issued, without more than perhaps a handful of people knowing the cause, tens of thousands of people whose identity could not be ascertained by months of investigation, are made to use the material or its products more sparingly.
>
> — Friedrich A. Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945)

A price puts into one signal knowledge that nobody has in full. Thus, millions of people coordinate their actions without a central direction (see [Trade and Division of Labor](08-trade-and-division-of-labor.md)).

## The Pretense of Knowledge

Hayek returned to these ideas in his Nobel lecture of 1974, "The Pretence of Knowledge." He criticizes scientism, which is the imitation of the methods of the physical sciences in the study of complex phenomena such as the economy. His target was the macroeconomics of his time, mainly the assumed correlation between aggregate demand and total employment. In complex phenomena, the quantitative data that economists can get are limited, and they may not include the important facts. But economists accept the relations for which they have data as the only causal relations that count, and they ignore what they cannot observe, such as the dispersed and tacit knowledge of individuals. Hayek writes that this imitation of the physical sciences "may lead to outright error."

Hayek does not ask economists to stop the use of theory. He asks them to be modest about what theory can do. In place of specific, quantitative predictions, economics can make "pattern predictions": predictions of the general properties of a complex system, but not of its specific results. A person who thinks that he has knowledge that he does not have hopes to "mould society entirely to our liking." Hayek warns that this attempt to control society can make the planner a tyrant, and can destroy a civilization. He recommends a different attitude:

> He will therefore have to use what knowledge he can achieve, not to shape the results as the craftsman shapes his handiwork, but rather to cultivate a growth by providing the appropriate environment, in the manner in which the gardener does this for his plants.
>
> — Friedrich A. Hayek, "The Pretence of Knowledge" (1974)
