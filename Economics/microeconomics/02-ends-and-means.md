# Ends and Means

Each [human action](01-human-action.md) has two parts. The end is the goal that the actor wants to reach. The means are the things that he uses to reach it. Economics does not judge the ends. It studies how people use means to reach them.

## Ends

An end is a state of affairs that the actor prefers to the present state. Ends have two properties.

1. **Unlimited.** Humans always have more ends than they can reach. When a person reaches one end, a new uneasiness appears, and he wants something else. A person who has food wants better food, then a house, then education for his children, then free time. Nobody reaches a state where all his wants are satisfied.
2. **Subjective.** Each person ranks his ends in a different order. The value of an end is the priority that the actor gives to it, and this priority exists only in his mind. Two people can want the same thing with different intensity, and the same person can value it differently at different times. Values are ranked, not measured (see [Marginalism](04-marginalism.md)). A person knows that he prefers A to B, but he cannot say by how many units.

## Means

A means is anything that the actor thinks will help him reach his end. Means are also called economic goods. A thing is not a means because of its physical properties. It becomes a means when a person sees a use for it. Carl Menger gives four conditions for a thing to become a good:

1. A human need exists.
2. The thing has properties that can satisfy this need.
3. A person knows that the thing can satisfy the need.
4. A person has command of the thing, so he can use it to satisfy the need.

If one condition is missing, the thing is not a good. For example, a medicinal plant was not a good before people knew its effect. It became a good when people discovered this effect, but the plant itself did not change.

Means also have two properties.

1. **Scarce.** Means are limited in relation to the ends that people want to reach with them. If a person uses a resource for one end, he cannot use it for another end. This is the origin of the [opportunity cost](06-opportunity-cost.md) of each action. A thing that is available in quantities greater than all needs, such as the air in normal conditions, is not an object of action. Nobody needs to economize it.
2. **Subjectively useful.** The usefulness of a means is not a property of the thing. Each actor sees different uses for the same means, so each actor values it differently. A piece of land is a farm for one person, the site of a factory for a second person, and a park for a third person. The value of a means comes from the value of the ends that it helps to reach.

## Types of Goods

Economists classify goods in different ways. The main classifications come from Menger and Mises.

- **Free goods and economic goods.** A free good is available in quantities greater than all needs, so it has no price and nobody economizes it. An economic good is scarce, so people must choose between its uses. The same thing can change from one type to the other. Water is a free good next to a large river, but an economic good in a desert.
- **Consumer goods (goods of the first order).** These goods satisfy a need directly. Examples are bread, clothes, and a haircut.
- **Producer goods (goods of a higher order).** These goods do not satisfy a need directly. They help to make other goods. Menger uses bread as an example. Flour, the oven, and the labor of the baker are goods of the second order, because they make bread. Wheat and the mill are goods of the third order, because they make flour. A producer good gets its value from the value of the consumer goods that it helps to make.
- **Complementary goods.** Many goods are useful only together with other goods. Flour without an oven, fuel, and a baker does not make bread. To produce a good, a person must have all its complementary goods at the same time.
- **Material goods and services.** A good does not need to be a physical object. The work of a doctor, a lesson, or a concert also satisfies a need, so they are also goods.
- **Monetary goods.** A monetary good is a good that people hold because they can exchange it for other goods. Mises explains that money is neither a consumer good nor a producer good. It is a medium of exchange, not an end.

## The Economic Problem

Means are scarce and subjectively useful. Millions of people pursue an unlimited number of ends, and each person ranks these ends differently. How can a society use its scarce means so that people reach the largest number of their most valued ends? This is the main question of economics.

Lionel Robbins gave a similar definition of economics, as the study of the relation between ends and scarce means (see [Definition of Economics](00-definition-of-economics.md)). The Austrian school adds that nobody knows all the ends and all the means. Each person knows only his own ends and his local circumstances. Friedrich Hayek explains that the economic problem is "a problem of the utilization of knowledge which is not given to anyone in its totality." Thus, no central planner can solve the problem. Prices, private property, and entrepreneurs solve it, because they let each person use his own knowledge and coordinate his plans with the plans of others.
