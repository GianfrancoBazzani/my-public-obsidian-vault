# Opportunity Cost

> The scarcity of time is why humans have to think not just about direct monetary costs associated with any activity, but about its opportunity cost: the cost of an activity in terms of the forgone value of a different activity in which a person could have engaged. The fact that our time is scarce means we cannot engage in all activities at all times. We must choose. Even if physical resources were not a constraint, the time needed to carry out activities is always a constraint, and humans must factor in the alternatives they forego every time they partake in an activity.
>
> — Saifedean Ammous, *Principles of Economics* (2023), ch. 3, p. 47

To understand the economic decisions of people, we must take into account their opportunity cost. When an individual evaluates the next-best alternative that each choice makes him give up, he can make better decisions and get the most value from his actions. In practice, this means that he economizes his time and resources.

## The Next-Best Alternative

Every choice has many alternatives, but only one of them is the opportunity cost. The cost of an action is the value of the best alternative that the individual gives up, not the sum of all the alternatives that he does not choose.

## Cost Is Subjective

In the Austrian view, opportunity cost is subjective. Mises explains that action sorts and grades its ends, so values are ranked in order and not measured with numbers. Only the individual knows how he ranks his alternatives, and two people who face the same options can have different costs. For this reason, nobody can observe or measure the opportunity cost of another person. An observer sees only the choice, not the alternative that the actor gave up.

James Buchanan adds that cost exists only in the mind of the decision maker and only at the moment of choice. The cost is the value of an alternative that will never happen, so it disappears when the choice is made. After the choice, nobody can know with certainty what the forgone alternative would have given.

## Monetary Calculation as a Tool

Money prices help individuals to estimate their opportunity costs. If the paid shift of the student pays 80 euros, he knows that study costs him at least the things that he could buy with 80 euros. Monetary calculation makes it possible to compare alternatives that are very different, such as an hour of work and an hour of rest. But the money price is only a tool for the estimate. The real cost is the value that the individual gives to the forgone alternative. If he values the evening with his friends more than 80 euros, then the cost of study is higher than the money price shows.

## Sunk Costs

Opportunity cost looks forward, to the alternatives that are still open. Resources that the individual already spent and cannot recover are sunk costs. They are not part of the opportunity cost of a new decision, because no present choice can change them. For example, if a person paid for a concert ticket and then feels ill, the price of the ticket is the same if he goes or if he stays at home. The correct decision compares only the evening at the concert with the evening at home. To include the sunk cost in the decision is a common error, and it can make people continue with actions that no longer give them value.
