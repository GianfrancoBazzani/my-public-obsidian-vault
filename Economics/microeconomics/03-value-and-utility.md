# Value and Utility

> Value is thus nothing inherent in goods, no property of them, nor an independent thing existing by itself. It is a judgment economizing men make about the importance of the goods at their disposal for the maintenance of their lives and well-being. Hence value does not exist outside the consciousness of men.
>
> — Carl Menger, *Principles of Economics* (1871), ch. III, §1

## Value and Utility

What is economic value? In [Ends and Means](02-ends-and-means.md), we saw that value is the subjective priority that an actor gives to an end, the intensity with which he wants to reach it. A means is anything that the actor thinks is useful to reach his end.

Utility is the capacity of a means to satisfy a need. It is the subjective importance that the actor gives to a means, because of the value of the end that the means helps him to reach. Value and utility are two sides of the same coin. The actor values his end, and he projects this value onto the means that he thinks useful to reach it. Utility is the name of this projection.

Menger defines value as "the importance that individual goods or quantities of goods attain for us because we are conscious of being dependent on command of them for the satisfaction of our needs." Thus, we first value the satisfaction of our needs, and then we carry this value over to the goods that satisfy them.

## How Value Is Determined

Two factors determine the value of a good.

1. **Subjective utility.** Utility is subjective, because it depends on the end of the actor and on his belief that the good can help him reach it. Utility is also marginal. The actor values each unit of a good by the least important use of his stock (see [Marginalism](04-marginalism.md)).
2. **Economic scarcity.** A good is economically scarce when the quantity that people want is greater than the quantity available. A physically rare thing, such as an old letter of an unknown person, can have no value because nobody wants it. A physically abundant thing, such as water in a desert city, can be economically scarce.

Randy Barnett explains why economic scarcity is not the same as physical scarcity. For him, economic scarcity is subjective. Imagine a world with an unlimited number of trees. One tree stands between my house and the house of my neighbor. I want to keep it, because I like to look at it. My neighbor wants to cut it, because it blocks her view of the sunset. The unlimited number of trees does not solve our problem, because the relevant good is not "trees" but "the tree between our houses". Our two actions need the same object for different uses at the same time, so they cannot both happen.

Thus, a good is economically scarce when one or more persons want to use it, and it cannot satisfy all these wants at the same time. This is the same definition as above. Scarcity comes from a conflict between wants, not from the physical quantity of a thing. It can also exist for one person who wants to use a resource for two incompossible uses. In a world where all the wanted uses of resources were compatible, resources could be as physically limited as in our world, but nothing would be economically scarce.

The more useful and the more scarce a good is, the more people value it. The less useful and the less scarce a good is, the less people value it. A good that is useful but not scarce, such as air, is a free good, and nobody needs to economize it (see [Ends and Means](02-ends-and-means.md)).

Scarcity forces people to economize. For Menger, to economize means four things:

- To keep the largest possible quantity of the goods that satisfy our needs.
- To conserve the useful qualities of these goods.
- To satisfy the most important needs first, and the less important needs later.
- To get the greatest satisfaction from a given quantity of goods.

## Subjective Value

Menger's most important discovery is that value is subjective. Value exists only in the mind of the person who values. It is not a physical or chemical property of goods. A good has value only because a person judges that it can satisfy his needs. When this judgment changes, the value changes, even if the good stays the same.

Saifedean Ammous gives the example of oil. Before the nineteenth century, oil in a piece of land reduced the value of the land, because the owner had to remove it before he could farm or build there. When people discovered that they could refine oil and burn it in engines, oil became one of the most valuable goods in the world. The oil did not change. Only the knowledge and the needs of people changed. Menger explains that "the value of goods arises from their relationship to our needs, and is not inherent in the goods themselves."

The same oil had a negative price for a short time in 2020. During the COVID-19 lockdowns, factories and transport stopped, and the storage tanks were almost full. On 20 April 2020, the price of US crude oil for May delivery fell to about −37 dollars per barrel. The owners of the contracts paid others to take the oil. Some days later, the price was positive again. The physical properties of oil did not change. The circumstances of the people who valued it changed.

Value is subjective, but it is real. Values direct the actions of people, and these actions direct the production, the consumption, and the use of all material goods. Before Menger, economists could not explain why some goods have more value than others. Subjective value and marginal analysis solved these problems, for example the water-diamond paradox.

## Ordinal and Cardinal Valuation

Value is subjective, so nobody can measure it. To measure a quantity, we need a constant unit that everybody accepts. We measure length in meters and weight in kilograms. No such unit exists for value. The value of a good depends on the person and on his changing circumstances, so a unit of value cannot be constant.

Thus, we can only rank values. This is ordinal valuation. A person knows that he prefers A to B, and B to C. The constant reference for this comparison is the person himself. But he cannot say that A is worth 14.37 units and B 4.26 units. This would be cardinal valuation, and it is not possible. Mises explains:

> There is a more and a less in the removal of uneasiness felt; but how much one satisfaction surpasses another one can only be felt; it cannot be established and determined in an objective way. A judgment of value does not measure, it arranges in a scale of degrees, it grades.
>
> — Ludwig von Mises, *Human Action* (1949), ch. IV, §4

Think of how you value your own things. You can say that you prefer your family to your car. But can you say how many cars your child is worth? Values can be compared, but they cannot be added, subtracted, or multiplied. Without a common and constant unit, measurement and mathematical operations are not possible.

There are two consequences.

1. **No interpersonal comparisons.** We cannot compare the satisfaction of one person with the satisfaction of another person, because there is no common unit.
2. **Prices do not measure value.** A price is an exchange ratio between two goods. It does not measure value. In a voluntary exchange, the buyer values the good more than its price, and the seller values it less than its price. If they valued it equally, they would not trade. Money prices make [economic calculation](07-economic-calculation.md) possible, but they do not measure the subjective value of goods.
