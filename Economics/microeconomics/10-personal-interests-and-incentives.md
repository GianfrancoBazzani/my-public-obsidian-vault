# Personal Interests and Incentives

## Personal Interest

Why do individuals perform economic actions? Because they pursue their own interests. Mises writes that "the incentive that impels a man to act is always some uneasiness", because a man perfectly content with his state of affairs would have no reason to change it. Uneasiness alone is not sufficient. The individual must also imagine a more satisfactory state of affairs, and he must expect that his action can remove or at least reduce the uneasiness. If one of these three conditions is missing, he does not act (see [Human Action](01-human-action.md)). It is important to note that personal interest does not mean selfishness. The ends that an individual chooses can be material or spiritual, and they can include the well-being of his family, his community, or strangers. What matters is that the ends are his own, because only the individual can rank his goals and only he can judge whether an action made him better off.

> It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest. We address ourselves, not to their humanity, but to their self-love, and never talk to them of our own necessities, but of their advantages.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), bk. I, ch. II

## Incentives

Incentives are the rewards and costs that an individual expects from each alternative that he has. When incentives change, the expected outcome of each alternative changes, and so can the action that the individual chooses. Individuals tend to respond to incentives in a predictable direction, but not in a predictable size. Mises explains that we can predict that, other things being equal, a fall in the demand for a good will lower its price, but we cannot predict the extent of this drop, because in the field of human action there are no constant relations. Sometimes the response even goes in the opposite direction. In a study of ten day-care centers in Haifa, Gneezy and Rustichini found that a fine for parents who picked up their children late increased the number of late parents, and the number did not fall when the fine was removed. The parents saw the fine as a price for extra time, not as a punishment.

Love, social recognition, fame, and sex are powerful incentives. Even when someone altruistically helps another person, he acts to reach his own ends, because the well-being of that person is one of his goals. Mises calls action "selfish" only in this formal sense, and he writes that "even an action directly aiming at the improvement of other people's conditions is selfish", because the actor finds it more satisfactory to help than not to help. This does not mean that altruism is a disguise for personal fulfillment or social recognition.

## Cooperation

An individual pursues his own interests, but he cannot reach most of his goals alone. In a society based on the division of labor, he reaches them through voluntary exchange with other people. Nobody is obliged to trade with him, so he must offer something that others value more than what they give in return. To do this, he must do three things.

1. Provide what other individuals want.
2. Provide it with the best possible quality and at the lowest possible price.
3. Make the other party better off with each exchange.

A voluntary exchange benefits both parties, because each party values what he receives more than what he gives. If this were not true, he would not trade. The individuals who best serve the wants of others are the ones who, through social cooperation, reach their own goals. Mises states this for the entrepreneur, who "can succeed only by best serving the consumers", because "his profit depends on the approval of his conduct by the consumers".

Individuals are moved by their own interests, but to satisfy them they must cooperate with others and satisfy the interests of others. Mises writes that people "do not cooperate under the division of labor because they love or should love one another. They cooperate because this best serves their own interests."

## Institutions

Personal interest does not always lead to cooperation. Sometimes the incentives are too weak, and sometimes they are too strong. Incentives are too weak when an individual cannot keep the benefits of his action. Incentives are too strong when an individual can push part of the costs of his action onto other people. Mises calls these two cases external economies and external costs.
 
The rules of a society decide whether personal interests and social interests point in the same direction. North defines institutions as "the rules of the game in a society or, more formally, \[...\] the humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction", and he explains that they structure the incentives in human exchange. Good institutions align personal interests with social interests.

- **Private property.** The owner gets the benefits and bears the costs of the use of his goods, so he has an incentive to use them well and to conserve them. Mises calls private ownership of the means of production "the fundamental institution of the market economy".
- **Freedom of contract.** Individuals are free to agree on the terms of an exchange. An exchange takes place only when both parties expect to benefit from it.
- **Free competition.** Any individual can offer a better or cheaper product. A producer keeps his customers only if he serves them better than his competitors do.
- **Rule of law.** Hayek describes the rule of law as a government that is bound in all its actions by rules fixed and announced in advance. With such rules, each individual can predict how the authorities will use their coercive power, and he can plan his own affairs.
- **Money.** Mises defines money as "the generally accepted and commonly used medium of exchange". Without money, an exchange is possible only when each party wants exactly what the other party offers. With money, each individual can sell what he produces to anyone and buy what he wants from anyone, so the circle of cooperation becomes much larger. Money also makes economic calculation possible, because prices in money show each individual what others value and which uses of resources are profitable. Menger explains that money was not invented by a government, but emerged spontaneously, when individuals in pursuit of their own interests started to accept the most marketable goods in exchange.

History supports this argument. Acemoglu and Robinson compare inclusive institutions, with secure property rights, an impartial system of law, and a level playing field for exchange and contracts, with extractive institutions, which enrich a small elite at the expense of the rest of society. In inclusive institutions, people keep most of the gains from their own efforts, so they have an incentive to work, save, invest, and innovate. In extractive institutions, this incentive is weak, and the economy does not grow.

## The Problem of Incentives

> Having knowledge of potential uses for resources is one thing. Putting that knowledge into action is another. Human action is costly.
>
> — Randy E. Barnett, *The Structure of Liberty* (1998), ch. 8

In *The Structure of Liberty* (1998), Randy Barnett explains in more detail why institutions such as property and contract give good incentives. Each person has personal and local knowledge that others do not have (see [The Problem of Knowledge](09-the-problem-of-knowledge.md)). But knowledge is not enough. A person must also want to act on it, and each action has a subjective cost that only he bears (see [Opportunity Cost](06-opportunity-cost.md)). Barnett calls this kind of difficulty a problem of interest, and the incentive problem is one of these problems.

Barnett defines the problem as follows:

> The Incentive Problem: ensuring that persons have an adequate incentive to make choices reflecting the knowledge to which they have access and to discover new information; it is the need to close the gap between the conduct that justice permits and a right-holder's interest to act knowledgeably with his or her resources.
>
> — Randy E. Barnett, *The Structure of Liberty* (1998), ch. 8

The problem has two parts:

1. **To use existing knowledge.** People must have an incentive to bear the cost of choice and to act on the knowledge that they already have.
2. **To discover new knowledge.** People must have an incentive to search for new knowledge about the uses of resources. This is entrepreneurship. Barnett follows Israel Kirzner: ignorance makes opportunities for profit possible, and the possibility of profit gives the entrepreneur the incentive to discover them.

### How Justice Gives Incentives

For Barnett, some of the rights that define justice solve the incentive problem:

- **First possession.** The first person who invests scarce resources to control an unowned resource becomes its owner. Without this right, nobody would improve a resource in a permanent way, because he could lose the benefits of his investment.
- **Freedom from contract.** Consent is necessary. Nobody can transfer the rights of a person without his consent, so nobody can take the results of his effort. This prevents negative incentives.
- **Freedom to contract.** Consent is sufficient. Agreements that people make in the hope of a profit are enforceable. This gives positive incentives for beneficial exchanges and makes entrepreneurship possible.
- **Stable rules.** Barnett adds that the legal rules must not change too frequently. As with the rule of law above, people must be able to rely on the rules, or they lose the incentive to invest and to conserve resources.

When someone takes the resources of a person without his consent, the incentives of that person decrease. To condemn the taking is not enough. The victim must receive compensation, and the person who took the resources must pay it. If someone else paid, a new forced transfer would happen. Barnett calls this the right of restitution.

### Free Riders

Some goods are nonexcludable. The producer cannot stop people who do not pay from using them. These people are free riders. This is a case of the external economies of Mises above: the producer cannot keep all the benefits of his action, so his incentive to produce the good is weak. The classic textbook example is a lighthouse, because its light helps all ships. Some economists conclude that the government must tax ship owners to pay for lighthouses.

Barnett answers that free riders are a smaller problem than people think. Ronald Coase discovered that, in England, ports paid for many lighthouses with port fees. Broadcast radio and television charge advertisers, not listeners. Free riders also give an incentive to invent ways of exclusion, such as cable television. A tax, in contrast, has problems of knowledge and of partiality. Without the consent of buyers and sellers, nobody can know how much people want a good. And nothing guarantees that the authority will use its power impartially.

### Knowledge and Incentives

The problem of knowledge and the problem of incentives are different. The first is about what people know. The second is about what people want to do. A completely honest and benevolent planner has no incentive problem, but he still cannot know the particular circumstances of millions of people. A planner who knows everything, but who acts in his own interest, has no knowledge problem, but he has an incentive problem. He does not bear the results of his decisions, so he has no reason to use resources for the good of their owners.

But the two problems are also connected. Local knowledge is useless if the person who has it has no reason to act on it. Good incentives are useless if the person who has them does not know what to do. Several property and freedom of contract solve both problems. They give the decision about each resource to the person who has the relevant knowledge, and they make this person bear the costs and receive the benefits of his decisions. Prices show this double function. In Hayek's example of tin (see [The Problem of Knowledge](09-the-problem-of-knowledge.md)), a higher price tells people that tin is scarcer. It also gives users a reason to use less tin, and gives producers a reason to produce more. A price is both a signal and a reward.
