# Transmission Channels and Pass-Through

The interest rate channel and the credit channel, with its balance sheet and bank lending parts, act through the cost of capital and the external finance premium (see [Interest Rate and Credit Channels](13-interest-rate-and-credit-channels.md)). Three more channels act through asset prices, risk-taking and the exchange rate. This note explains them, and then follows the policy rate to the rates that households and companies pay and receive.

## Asset Prices

Interest rates are the building blocks of asset prices. The buyer of a house looks at the rate of the mortgage, and the investor who values a share or a new project discounts its cash flows with an interest rate. A lower rate therefore raises asset prices. For a perpetuity that pays a cash flow $C$ each year, discounted at the rate $r$, the price is $P = C / r$.

$$P = \frac{5}{0.05} = 100 \qquad P = \frac{5}{0.04} = 125$$

A fall of the rate from 5% to 4% thus raises the price by 25%. Higher asset prices raise the net worth of households and companies and the value of their collateral, so the external finance premium falls through the balance sheet channel. A rise in rates works in reverse (see [Modern Money Creation](04-modern-money-creation.md)).

## Risk-Taking

The risk-taking channel is the effect of policy rates on how much risk investors and banks perceive and tolerate, and through it on the risk in their portfolios, on asset prices and on the terms of lending. When rates stay low for a long time, investors search for yield, which means that they buy riskier assets to earn the returns that they need. Risk spreads narrow, and the hierarchy of money flattens, as the liabilities of the lower layers trade as close substitutes for those of the layers above (see [Credit and Currency](03-credit-and-currency.md)).

A lower price of risk does not mean a lower risk. When some of the risks turn into losses, the losses spread through balance sheets and asset prices to a large part of the financial system, and the benign cycle turns into a negative feedback loop. This is the financial instability hypothesis of Hyman Minsky (see [Money Market Spreads](10-money-market-spreads.md)).

## Exchange Rate

A rise in the policy rate attracts capital, so the currency tends to appreciate. Exports become dearer and imports cheaper, which lowers the demand for domestic goods and lowers inflation directly. In ECB simulations, a 10% appreciation of the euro lowers inflation by up to 0.6 points after about a year and keeps it markedly lower for about three years. Under the classical gold standard, the Bank of England raised Bank rate for this reason, to stop the outflow of gold (see [Role of Central Banks](02-role-of-central-banks.md)).

## Pass-Through to Retail Rates

Pass-through is the transmission of the policy rate to the rates that banks charge and pay to households and companies, such as the rates of mortgages, consumer credit, business loans and deposits. Its speed depends on how past financing contracts were written.

### Fixed and Variable Rates

A variable-rate mortgage indexed to Euribor takes a rise at its next reset. The 12-month Euribor averaged −0.50% in December 2021 and 4.16% in October 2023, so the rate of a mortgage at Euribor plus one point, reset on these monthly averages, went from 0.50% to 5.16%. A 30-year fixed-rate mortgage never takes the rise, which reaches only new loans. New loans therefore react fast, but the rate on the stock of loans reacts only as fast as the share of variable rates allows.

From June 2022 to October 2023, the deposit facility rate of the ECB rose from −0.50% to 4.00%.

The rate on new mortgages rose in every country, by 1.55 points in the Netherlands to 3.13 points in Portugal. The rate on the stock rose by 2.38 to 3.67 points in Spain, Finland and Portugal, but by only about 0.2 points in Germany and France. The difference comes from the loans already in the stock. In a study of euro area countries for 2011 to 2015, variable rates made up only 7% to 21% of new mortgages in Belgium, Germany, France, the Netherlands and Slovakia, but 71% to 96% in the other countries. In the euro area as a whole, about a quarter of mortgages have purely variable rates, and a little over a third have rates fixed for more than ten years. In Germany and the Netherlands the rate on the stock has not fallen in any month since June 2022, through the cuts of 2024 and 2025, because old loans at low fixed rates keep maturing and new loans at higher rates replace them. In Germany it reached 2.42% in September 2026.

### Who Bears the Risk

A household with a fixed-rate mortgage does not feel a rise in rates, but the bank that lent at a fixed rate and funds the loan with short-term deposits does. The bank can hedge with an interest rate swap in which it pays a fixed rate and receives a floating rate (see [From LIBOR to SOFR and OIS](09-from-libor-to-sofr-and-ois.md)). In effect, its assets and liabilities then both pay floating rates, and the rise in rates hits the other side of the swap. A dealer on that side offsets the swap in turn with another client or dealer, as in a matched book (see [Matched Book and the September 2019 Repo Spike](07-matched-book-and-the-september-2019-repo-spike.md)).

A swap does not remove the risk, it moves it to another balance sheet. In the euro area, banks use interest rate swaps to spread the risk within the banking sector and to transfer it to insurers and pension funds. If the last holder of the risk has a matched book, with assets and liabilities that both pay floating rates, the system absorbs the rise in rates. If it does not, the loss spreads through the balance sheet and asset price channels, and it can reach the whole financial system. Silicon Valley Bank, which held long-term fixed-rate securities funded by deposits that could leave, failed in this way in March 2023 (see [Matched Book and the September 2019 Repo Spike](07-matched-book-and-the-september-2019-repo-spike.md)).

### Loans and Deposits

Pass-through also differs between instruments. Deposit rates react much more slowly than loan rates, and the rates on overnight deposits hardly move.

| Euro area rate | June 2022 | October 2023 | Change in points | Share of the rise of the deposit facility rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deposit facility rate | −0.50% | 4.00% | 4.50 | 100% |
| 12-month Euribor, monthly average | 0.85% | 4.16% | 3.31 | 74% |
| New loans to companies | 1.83% | 5.28% | 3.45 | 77% |
| New loans for house purchase | 1.97% | 3.92% | 1.95 | 43% |
| Stock of loans for house purchase | 1.62% | 2.38% | 0.76 | 17% |
| New household deposits with a maturity of up to one year | 0.23% | 3.29% | 3.06 | 68% |
| Overnight deposits of companies | −0.05% | 0.80% | 0.85 | 19% |
| Overnight deposits of households | 0.00% | 0.35% | 0.35 | 8% |

Banks raised the rates on overnight deposits little, partly because they needed little new funding. With abundant reserves (see [Monetary Policy Implementation](05-monetary-policy-implementation.md)) and weak demand for loans, they had little reason to compete for more deposits, and in some segments of the deposit market competition was weak. Excess liquidity is only part of the explanation, and its effect is hard to measure. Loan rates rose much more than deposit rates, so in this regime the margin of banks widened as rates rose.

## More Art Than Science

The channels feed one another, as in any complex system. A rise in rates lowers asset prices, which weakens balance sheets and raises the external finance premium. The channels can push in the same direction or in opposite directions. A rise in rates cuts the spending of borrowers, for example, but it raises the interest income of savers and, while deposit rates lag, the margins of banks. What happens after a change in the policy rate depends on the balance sheets of households, companies and banks, on the maturities at which they borrowed, and on what the market expects the central bank to do next. The analysis of each case is therefore more an art than a science, and it improves with practice.

## Summary of the Channels

The table sums up the five channels of a rise in the policy rate, the two of [Interest Rate and Credit Channels](13-interest-rate-and-credit-channels.md) and the three of this note.

| Channel | Actors | Where the impact comes from after a rise in the policy rate | What it hits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Interest rate, or cost of capital | Central bank, firms and households | The real rate rises while prices adjust slowly, so the cost of capital rises | Less business investment and less spending on housing, inventories and consumer durables |
| Credit, balance sheet | Borrowers and their lenders | The net worth, cash flow and collateral of borrowers weaken | A higher external finance premium, so less spending, most by small firms and households |
| Credit, bank lending | Banks, the wholesale lenders of banks and borrowers that depend on banks | The cost of bank funding rises, most for small banks | Less credit to borrowers that depend on banks, such as small firms |
| Asset prices | Investors, owners of houses and shares, and lenders that take collateral | The present value of future cash flows falls, so the prices of houses and shares fall | Lower wealth and less collateral, so less spending |
| Risk-taking | Investors, banks and riskier borrowers | The search for yield weakens, so investors and banks bear less risk | Wider risk spreads and less lending to riskier borrowers |
| Exchange rate | Domestic and foreign investors, exporters, importers and consumers | Capital flows in, so the currency appreciates | Dearer exports and cheaper imports, so lower net exports, import prices and inflation |
