# Evolutionary Emergence of Banks

In chapter 6, "The Market Makes its Money", of *A Market Theory of Money* (1989), Sir John Hicks (Nobel Memorial Prize, 1972) shows how a market of traders produces banks by itself. The market starts with coin as the standard of value and ends with its own money, which is the debt of banks. Hicks gives a logical sequence, not a dated history, so the table puts each step next to its counterpart in European history.

| Step | Logic of Hicks | Historical counterpart |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trade on credit | Only small trades are paid on the spot, so most trades create debts | Merchants settled their debts at the close of each Fair of Champagne (12th–13th centuries) |
| Set-off | Debts cancel each other or pass to a third party | Netting of bills among bankers at the fairs of Lyons and Bisanzone (16th century) |
| Circles of trust | A promise to pay is accepted near face value only by traders who know the debtor | A merchant who failed to settle was banished from future fairs |
| Guarantors | A trader in two circles guarantees a debt for a fee | The payer accepted the medieval bill, and London accepting houses guaranteed bills (19th century) |
| Discounters | Dealers buy bills for cash below face value | Money dealers of Antwerp (after 1541) and bill brokers of London (19th century) |
| Banks | Discounters borrow from custodians of coin, and deposits become money | Money changers of Italy and goldsmiths of London who took deposits |

## Sequence of Hicks

**Trade on Credit and Set-Off**

A trade has three steps, which are the contract, the delivery, and the payment. The gap between contract and payment is credit, and it creates a debt in money. Money is thus the standard of value and the means of payment, but a debt can also be settled without money. Two debts between A and B cancel each other. B can also pay A with a debt that C owes to B, but A accepts only if A trusts C, so "there is a question of trust, or confidence, as soon as a third party is brought in".

**Circles of Trust**

The value of a promise to pay depends on the reputation of the debtor, so the traders who know the debtor value it more. Each trader thus has a circle of traders who accept the promises of the trader at full face value or near it, and inside the circle debts cancel freely. Circles overlap. If C is outside the circle of A but inside the circle of D, and D is inside the circle of A, then A accepts the debt of C with the guarantee of D, and D charges A for this service.

**Dealers in Debts**

As the market grows, two types of specialist appear.

- **Guarantors** sell acceptances. They know many debtors, or know where to buy an acceptance from someone who knows them.
- **Discounters** pay cash for accepted bills, below face value.

The discount pays for risk and for time. Bills of equal reliability still differ in maturity, because a trader in need of cash needs it now. So "there is a discount on a prime bill which is a pure matter of time preference – a pure rate of interest". A prime bill of 100 due in three months that sells for 98 pays about 2% for three months, or about 8% a year. Hicks adds that a competitive market for plain loans cannot exist without the same confidence in the future ability of the borrower to pay.

**From Dealers in Debts to Banks**

When trade expands, more bills come in for discount. One dealer can get cash when other dealers discount the bills of this dealer, but the dealers as a group cannot. "They must get cash from outside the market. They themselves must become borrowers." The cash comes from the custodians who keep the coin of the public. A firm that lends to the market and borrows as a custodian from the public is a bank.

Deposits then become money in two ways. A transferable receipt for a deposit can pay a debt, as a banknote does, and an order of the depositor to the bank can pay a debt, as a check does. When the bank lends, it can credit the borrower with a deposit and keep its coin, so "from the point of view of the rest of the economy, the bank has 'created' money". The debt of the bank, the firm at the center of the widest circle of trust, becomes the money of the market.

## Bills of Exchange and Foreign Exchange

**Origin**

1. Late 12th century. In the trade between northern Italy and the Fairs of Champagne, merchants start to move money with the *cambium* contract, a notarial promise to pay in another place and currency. The earliest surviving record dates from 1220.
2. Late 13th century. Trading companies with permanent branches replace the traveling merchants and use the bill of exchange, an informal letter that orders a payment.
3. End of the 14th century. The bill is the main instrument of remittance between Italy and its trading partners.

Coin was heavy and risky to ship. In 1328, half of the 60,000 florins that Pope John XXII sent to his army in Lombardy was lost in an ambush, despite an escort of 150 horsemen. When Henry VIII had to send £20,000 to Swiss mercenaries in coin, the cost rose by about £2,000, or 10%.

**Mechanics**

The deliverer gave local coin to the taker. The taker ordered a correspondent, the payer, to pay a correspondent of the deliverer, the payee, in another city and currency. The payer accepted the bill by writing *acettata* on its face, and after the acceptance the payee could sue the payer. If the payer refused, a notary recorded a protest. The bill came due after the usance, a time fixed by custom for each route. Around 1400, the usance from Venice was 20 days to Florence, two months to Bruges, and three months to London, longer than the post (6, 26, and 33 days). Each bill was thus a loan and a currency exchange in one contract.

**Usance Rate as Spot Rate and Interest**

The market quoted usance rates, which combined a notional spot rate with the interest rates of the two places. Let $S$ be the spot rate in groats of Antwerp per pound sterling, and $i_A$ and $i_L$ the one-month interest rates in Antwerp and London.

| Bill | Usance rate |
| --- | --- |
| Groats paid in Antwerp today for £1 in London in one month | $R_A = S/(1+i_L)$ |
| £1 paid in London today for groats in Antwerp in one month | $R_L = S(1+i_A)$ |
| Round trip, out and back | $R_L/R_A = (1+i_A)(1+i_L)$ |

The gap between the two usance rates is thus the interest on a two-month loan. Today the spot and forward markets are separate, and covered interest parity gives the forward rate $F = S(1+i_A)/(1+i_L)$. A currency exchange in time still contains a loan.

**Usury and Dry Exchange**

The return of a bill depended on a future exchange rate, so the Church treated it as a profit on exchange, not as interest. Dry exchange used this to make pure loans. A bill went out and a second bill came back, which left a loan with a maturity of twice the usance, for example six months from Venice to London and back. The usury ban was an advantage of the bill but not its main reason, and the bill stayed popular after the ban weakened in the 16th century. Already in 1336–1340, only 70 of the 443 exchange deals of the Covoni company of Florence came from trade, and 335 used the difference in interest rates between Florence and Venice.

**Money Changers as Bankers**

The exchange met near the benches of the money changers and notaries, and the word bank comes from the Old Italian *banco*, the table or counter of these dealers. In Florence, cash settlement took place at the assay office of the Arte del Cambio, the guild of the money changers. The cities with an exchange market, the banking places, formed a network from Italy and Champagne to Bruges, London, and Antwerp. In 1609, the city of Amsterdam founded the Wisselbank, an exchange bank, because about 500 kinds of coin circulated. Its bank money gave a stable means to settle bills, and bills on Amsterdam became a liquid asset across Europe.

## Circles of Trust in History

| Market | Participants | Settlement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inside market (Lyons, Bisanzone) | Few merchant bankers of known reputation | Direct netting of their own debts. At Lyons, a million livres tournois changed hands in one morning without a single coin. |
| Public market (Genoa, Venice, Bruges) | Strangers | Transfer of deposits in a bank. In 1456–1459, one bank in Genoa settled 92.5% of 160,000 lire of foreign bills in bank and 7.5% in coin. |
| Antwerp, after the ban of deposit banks in the 1480s | Strangers | Transfer of negotiable paper |

Exclusion enforced the trust. At the early fairs, a merchant who failed to settle was declared bankrupt, lost all credit, and was banished from future fairs. In Antwerp, the paper of a firm of impeccable credit, a *Ditta di Borsa*, circulated freely, like the prime bill of Hicks.

Antwerp extended trust to strangers in steps.

1. 1507. The civil courts recognize the transfer of a letter obligatory, a written debt, to its bearer.
2. 1536 and 1541. Edicts of Charles V make this paper negotiable, so an unpaid holder can claim from each earlier holder.
3. 1540s. Discount is allowed up to 12% interest, and after 1541 money dealers discount paper before it is due.
4. 1570s. Each holder signs the back of the bill. This endorsement makes each signer a guarantor.

Paper then changed hands ten or twenty times before it was due, sometimes a hundred times, and the paper of the Fugger passed almost like banknotes. The discount of negotiable bills became the base of commercial banking in the 17th and 18th centuries. In 19th-century London, the two dealers of Hicks were separate firms. Accepting houses, such as Rothschild and Schroders, guaranteed bills, and bill brokers bought them with money that they borrowed from banks against the bills.

## Lending to Kings and Governments

The money market of the bills was a main source of credit for governments too, which also used bills to pay armies abroad, subsidies, ransoms, and dowries. A merchant who does not pay loses the circle of trust and can be sued. A king controls the courts, so the only weapon of the lenders is to refuse new loans. Hicks treats sovereign finance in chapter 6, "The Finances of the Sovereign", of *A Theory of Economic History* (1969).

| Period | Borrower | Lenders | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1330s–1340s | Edward III of England, for the war with France | Bardi and Peruzzi of Florence | The king cut the debts after audits, paid part in cash and wool, and never paid the rest. Both firms failed in the 1340s. But the claimed loans of 900,000 and 600,000 florins exceeded their capital, and the Acciaiuoli, who did not lend to Edward, failed too. |
| 1519 | Charles I of Spain, for his election as emperor | Fugger (about 550,000 of 850,000 guilders), Welser, and Italian bankers | Charles won. In 1523, Jakob Fugger reminded him that he could not have won the crown without the help of Fugger. |
| 1557–1596 | Philip II of Spain | Genoese bankers | Four stops of payment (1557, 1560, 1575, 1596). Syndicates kept the bankers united, so a stop cut the king off from all of them. Lending went on, and 89% of the loans after 1575 were repeat business. |
| 1672 | Charles II of England | London goldsmith bankers, who bought crown debt at a discount for 8 to 10% a year | Stop of the Exchequer (2 January 1672) on about £1.2 million of debt. Many goldsmith bankers were ruined. |
| 1694 | William III, for the Nine Years' War | 1,268 subscribers | A loan of £1.2 million at 8%, plus £4,000 a year for management, raised in 11 to 12 days. The subscribers became the Bank of England. |

The Genoese lent with bills on Antwerp, paid in gold to the Spanish army, and took their repayment in Spain in silver from the Americas. From 1567 to 1632, this system sent more than 500 million florins to the armies in the Low Countries.

The Revolution of 1688 changed the borrower. Parliament now voted the taxes and stood behind the loans, so the king could no longer default alone. The Bank of England could deal in bills of exchange and bullion, took deposits, and issued banknotes, so it joined the lender to the government with the bank of Hicks. Still, the risk premium on British government debt stayed high for some decades after 1688. The bank at the center of the money market, which is also the lender to the government, is the origin of the central bank, the subject of [the next note](02-role-of-central-banks.md).

## Limits of the Account

The sequence of Hicks is a model, and history did not follow it step by step. Remittance came first and credit second, deposit banks grew from the money changers in parallel with the bill market, and cities such as Amsterdam founded public banks. History does support the core of the model. Trust started in closed circles of merchants, guarantees, negotiability, endorsement, and discount extended it to strangers, and banks emerged at the center of these circles.
