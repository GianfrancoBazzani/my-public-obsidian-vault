# Role of Central Banks

## The Problem of Bank Runs

A bank that lends its deposits has a maturity mismatch. Depositors can withdraw at any time, but the bank cannot collect its loans before they come due. A run is thus self-fulfilling. If each depositor expects the others to withdraw, the best choice is to withdraw first, and this expectation alone can break a bank that is otherwise sound.

In normal times, a small reserve of base money is enough. The bank uses it to pay withdrawals and to settle debts with other banks. Inflows and outflows mostly cancel each other, by the law of large numbers.

In a run, a sound bank has three exits.

1. Sell assets.
2. Borrow from other banks.
3. Stop new loans and ask borrowers for early payment.

Each exit works only when one bank is in trouble. In a general panic, the other banks also lose deposits, so nobody buys the assets or lends. Borrowers can pay early only if somebody else pays or lends to them. The sudden loss of credit then hurts the economy, which in turn increases the distrust that caused the run. Wilhelm Röpke called this cumulative contraction, out of proportion to the preceding boom, a secondary depression.

There is also a coordination problem. With many banks and no leader, each bank protects itself. It cuts loans, holds more reserves, and lets the others keep up the volume of credit. When all banks do this at once, financial and economic conditions get worse together. After 9 August 2007, for example, the demand for liquidity of the large UK settlement banks rose by 30%, and this pushed up the overnight interbank rates.

## Legal Reserve Requirements

The first solution in the United States was a legal minimum reserve. The National Bank Act of 1864 set the reserve at 25% of notes and deposits for the banks of 17 large cities, and at 15% for the other banks.

- The other banks could keep three fifths of their reserve as deposits at banks of the 17 cities, and these banks could keep half of theirs in New York. In a panic, each bank drew down its deposits at the banks above it.
- A bank below the minimum could not make new loans or pay dividends. If it did not restore the reserve within 30 days of a notice, the Comptroller of the Currency, with the Secretary of the Treasury, could appoint a receiver.

These reserves did not work as a buffer, because one dollar cannot both pay a depositor and meet the requirement. In 1922, Dennis Robertson wrote about this law that "an iron ration which you must not touch even in the throes of starvation is something of a mockery".

The problem returned in March 2020. Regulators had to tell banks explicitly that they could use the buffers that regulation had made them build.

- **Fed.** On 15 March 2020, it encouraged banks to use their capital and liquidity buffers and cut reserve requirements to zero, from 26 March. On 17 March, the Fed, the FDIC, and the OCC issued a joint statement on the use of these buffers.
- **ECB.** On 12 March 2020, it allowed banks to operate temporarily below the Pillar 2 Guidance, the capital conservation buffer, and the liquidity coverage ratio.

## Organic Emergence of Central Banking

A reserve of cash and of lending capacity somewhere in the financial system is useful in an emergency. Where the state did not intervene, banks built private institutions with some of these functions.

**Clearing Houses in the United States**

From 1863 to 1913, New York, the money center of the United States, had eight banking panics. In 1873, 1893, and 1907, the panic spread to the whole country, and the banks partially suspended cash payments. With no central bank, the clearing houses took its role.

- The New York Clearing House started on 11 October 1853 to settle checks among its members.
- Its loan certificates originated in the panic of 1857, and the first issue was on 23 November 1860. A member pledged its assets and got certificates that the other members accepted in settlement, and all members were jointly liable for them.
- In 1860 and 1873, the members also pooled their reserves as a common fund.
- During a panic, the clearing house published only the total of its members, not the data of each bank. The banks thus acted almost as a single firm, which in effect insured the deposits of all of them.

**Suffolk System in New England**

From 1825 to 1858, the Suffolk Bank of Boston cleared the banknotes of the New England banks. Each country bank kept with it a permanent deposit of $2,000 for each $100,000 of capital, plus a deposit to redeem its notes. The notes of all New England banks thus circulated at par in the whole region.

**Free Banking in Scotland**

From 1716, when the monopoly of the Bank of Scotland ended, to the Acts of 1844 and 1845, Scotland had free banking and no central bank of its own. From 1771, the banks exchanged and settled each other's notes at least once a week. In 1841, the losses of the public from all Scottish bank failures to that date were estimated at only £32,000, half the losses in London in 1840 alone.

The interpretation is under debate. One view holds that each bank kept its own reserves. The opposite view holds that the smaller banks relied on the reserves of the three chartered banks of Edinburgh, which in turn relied on the Bank of England.

**Panic of 1907**

In 1907, the lender of last resort of one of the largest economies in the world was a private banker, J. P. Morgan, then 70 years old. The panic started in the trust companies, which were not members of the New York Clearing House.

1. Morgan refused to help the Knickerbocker Trust, because nobody could confirm its solvency in time, and it closed on 22 October.
2. On the night of Saturday 2 November, he gathered the presidents of the trust companies in his library and locked the doors.
3. The doors opened only at 4:45 a.m., after they agreed to lend $25 million to the Trust Company of America.

## From Private Arrangement to State Institution

In 1873, Walter Bagehot wrote that "the natural system—that which would have sprung up if Government had let banking alone—is that of many banks of equal or not altogether unequal size". The origin of the central bank is still under debate.

- **Natural evolution.** Charles Goodhart holds that a non-competitive central bank that supervises the banks fulfils a necessary and natural function, and that the clearing house had a key role in its evolution.
- **Government favour.** Vera Smith holds that "a central bank is not a natural product of banking development. It is imposed from outside or comes into being as the result of Government favours."

**Bank of England**

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1694 | It was founded to lend £1.2 million to the government, in exchange for privileges that included the issue of banknotes. |
| 1797 | On 26 February, the government ordered it to stop the payment of its notes in gold. Payment in gold returned on 1 May 1821. |
| 1844 | The Bank Charter Act gave it an effective monopoly of new notes. Above a fiduciary issue of £14 million, each note needed the same value in gold. |
| 1854 | The London banks started to settle their clearing balances across their accounts at the Bank. |
| 1946 | The state nationalised it and got the power to appoint its governors and directors. |
| 1997 | It got operational independence in monetary policy in May. |

**Federal Reserve**

After 1907, the political power, and not the bankers, designed the central bank.

1. The Aldrich-Vreeland Act of 30 May 1908 created the National Monetary Commission.
2. The Federal Reserve Act became law on 23 December 1913.
3. The twelve Reserve Banks opened on 16 November 1914, a few months after the start of the First World War.

The Fed soon served the Treasury.

- **First World War.** From 1917, it lent to member banks against government securities at a preferential rate, about 0.5 percentage points below the rate on commercial paper, to help the sale of war bonds.
- **Second World War.** From 1942, it held the yield on Treasury bills at 3/8% until 1947, and the yield on long-term bonds at a maximum of 2.5% until the Treasury-Fed Accord of 4 March 1951.

The Fed is private only in form. The member banks must hold stock in their Reserve Bank, but they cannot sell or pledge it, and its dividend is at most 6%. The Board of Governors is a federal agency, and the President appoints its members with the confirmation of the Senate.

**Counterexample of Canada**

Canada had no central bank until the Bank of Canada opened in March 1935, as a private company with shares sold to the public. The state took it over in 1938. From 1914, the Finance Act let the government lend Dominion notes to the banks against securities, which made the government a lender of last resort.

In the Great Depression, no Canadian bank failed, while about 9,000 US banks suspended operations from 1930 to 1933. At the end of 1932, Canada had 10 banks with 3,158 branches. At the end of 1929, the United States had 24,633 banks, most of them small local banks.

## Central Banks in Normal Times

In normal times, the classical central bank did very little.

**Bank Rate as a Ceiling**

The Bank of England set its discount rate, the Bank rate, usually well above the market rate. The market came to the Bank only when the market rate rose to the Bank rate, so the Bank rate was a ceiling.

The Bank discounted only prime bills, which had no doubt of payment. This protected the value of its notes and their convertibility into gold. The Bank did not plan to sell these bills in a crisis, because a central bank that sells assets in a crisis does the opposite of its job. For the same reason, it kept a large reserve.

Since 1844, the Issue Department issued the notes, and the Banking Department kept a reserve of notes and coin against its deposits. This reserve was on average 43% of the liabilities of the Banking Department in the 1880s, and 48% from 1891 to 1913.

**Bank Rate in a Boom**

In a boom, or when gold left the country, the Bank raised the Bank rate. This had two purposes.

- **Domestic.** It reduced the demand for credit.
- **External.** It moved the exchange rates, which stopped the outflow of gold and attracted gold from abroad.

Central banks of emerging countries still use the external effect to defend a falling currency. The Bank of Russia raised its key rate from 10.5% to 17% on 16 December 2014, and the Central Bank of Argentina raised its rate from 45% to 60% on 30 August 2018.

**Draining the Market**

A ceiling has no effect if the market rate stays below it. When the banks had surplus funds, the Bank of England made its rate effective with a precursor of open market operations. It borrowed in the market, mainly against Consols, and thus drained the balances of the other banks.

The large structure of credit of London rested on a narrow base of bankers' balances at the Bank, about £22 to 23 million in 1909. The banks treated these balances as cash. When the Bank drained a part of them, the banks had to cut their loans by a multiple of that amount to restore their usual ratio of balances to deposits.

**Scarce and Ample Reserves**

The Fed worked with this system of scarce reserves until 2008. In October 2008, it started to pay interest on reserves, and in January 2019 it formally adopted a system of ample reserves. The gold standard added a second limit, because gold, the final means of settlement, was also scarce.

## Central Banks in a Crisis

In a crisis, the central bank had two duties. It had to keep its notes convertible into gold, which modern central banks no longer do, and to keep the banking system from a collapse, which they still do. These duties can require opposite actions, so central banking is closer to an art than to a science, as the title of the 1932 book of Ralph Hawtrey, *The Art of Central Banking*, says.

**Learning of the Bank of England**

The Bank learned slowly, and made errors in both directions. Two laws limited its options.

- **Usury laws.** Until 1833, they capped the rate on bills at 5%. The Bank could not charge a penalty rate, so it rationed credit and lent mainly to its regular customers.
- **Bank Charter Act of 1844.** To issue notes beyond the legal limit, the Bank needed a letter from the Prime Minister and the Chancellor of the Exchequer, which promised a law of indemnity.

| Crisis | Peak Bank rate | Rise of loans on private securities | Action of the Bank |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1793 and 1797 | 5%, the legal maximum | — | It cut its notes and lent too little. |
| 1825 | 5%, the legal maximum | — | It was slow at first, then lent "by every possible means". |
| 1847 | 8%, below the market rate | 8% | It rationed credit. The letter alone stopped the panic, and the Bank stayed within the limit. |
| 1857 | 10%, above the market rate | 54% | Unlike in 1847 and 1866, it issued notes beyond the limit. At most £928,000 of the extra notes circulated. |
| 1866 | 10%, above the market rate | 81% | It lent freely and stayed within the limit. |

**Bagehot's Rule**

The term lender of last resort comes from Francis Baring, who in 1797 called the Bank of England "the dernier resort". Henry Thornton stated most of the principles of the lender of last resort in 1802. Walter Bagehot made them a rule of action after the failure of Overend, Gurney & Co. on 10 May 1866, in *Lombard Street* (1873). In his words, "very large loans at very high rates are the best remedy for the worst malady of the money market".

| Element | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Lend freely, "as largely as the public ask" | Stop the panic, because a refusal to a borrower with good security spreads alarm. The promise to lend calms the public, as in 1847, so the central bank often has to lend less. |
| At a high rate | Make the loan "a heavy fine on unreasonable timidity", so that only banks in real need borrow. The high rate also protects the reserve and attracts gold from abroad. |
| On good collateral, "all good banking securities" | Protect the central bank from losses, which can cause an outflow of gold and a run on itself. A refusal of bad securities does not make the panic worse, because the "unsound" people are "a feeble minority". |
| Only to solvent banks | Solve problems of liquidity, not of solvency. |

The last element is under debate. Bagehot tested the collateral, not the borrower. Some economists read the condition of solvency into his rule, and others reply that, in a panic, it is not possible to tell an illiquid bank from an insolvent one.

The high rate is the element that modern central banks often forget. The penalty of the discount rate of the Fed, its primary credit rate, fell to zero in steps.

| From | Spread over the target for the federal funds rate |
|---|---|
| 9 January 2003 | 1 percentage point |
| 17 August 2007 | 0.5 percentage points |
| 16 March 2008 | 0.25 percentage points |
| 19 February 2010 | 0.5 percentage points over the top of the target range |
| 16 March 2020 | Zero, equal to the top of the target range |

From December 2007 to March 2010, the Fed also auctioned loans through its Term Auction Facility, at a rate set by the auction, to avoid the stigma of the discount window.

**Coordination of Private Help**

A central bank can also organize a private rescue, as J. P. Morgan did in 1907.

- **Barings, 1890.** On Saturday 15 November 1890, the Bank of England announced a guarantee fund of the London banks. It reached £17.1 million, with £1 million from the Bank itself. The fund guaranteed the Bank against losses on its advances to Barings, while the assets of Barings were sold in order. The Bank also obtained £3 million of gold from the Banque de France and £1.5 million from Russia.
- **LTCM, 1998.** On 23 September 1998, at a meeting that the Federal Reserve Bank of New York organized, 14 banks and brokers agreed to put $3.625 billion in the hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management. No public money was used.
