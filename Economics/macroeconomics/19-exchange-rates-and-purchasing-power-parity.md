# Exchange Rates and Purchasing Power Parity

Almost all the dollars in the world depend in the end on the Fed (see [Eurodollars and Central Bank Swap Lines](17-eurodollars-and-central-bank-swap-lines.md)). The dollar is money, but it also has a price in other currencies. In the hierarchy of money, three prices link the layers, which are par, the interest rate and the exchange rate (see [Credit and Currency](03-credit-and-currency.md)). This note explains the exchange rate from the market for goods, the oldest and most intuitive approach. Money and reserves (see [Balance of Payments and the Monetary Approach](20-balance-of-payments-and-the-monetary-approach.md)) and interest rates (see [Interest Rate Parity and Overshooting](21-interest-rate-parity-and-overshooting.md)) give two other approaches.

## The Exchange Rate

The exchange rate $e$ is the number of units of domestic currency that buy one unit of foreign currency, for example the number of euros that one dollar costs. When $e$ rises, the domestic currency depreciates, and when $e$ falls, it appreciates. A higher exchange rate therefore means a weaker domestic currency, not a stronger one. Many quotes use the opposite convention. The ECB, for example, quotes its reference rates in units of foreign currency per euro. On 26 November 2026 one euro was worth 1.0975 dollars, so one dollar cost about 0.91 euros.

The exchange rate matters for three reasons.

- **Inflation.** A depreciation makes imports dearer in domestic currency (see [Transmission Channels and Pass-Through](14-transmission-channels-and-pass-through.md)).
- **Trade balance.** A depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home.
- **Income distribution.** A devaluation helps exporters and hurts importers and anyone with debts in a foreign currency.

Interest groups therefore lobby over the exchange rate, and they often present their own interest as the interest of the whole country. In a large currency area such as the euro, no member state has its own exchange rate, so this temptation disappears at the national level.

## The Law of One Price

If the same good can be bought in two places, it should have the same price in both, measured in the same currency. If it does not, arbitrage appears. Someone buys the good where it is cheap, sells it where it is dear and keeps the difference. Arbitrage thus removes differences in prices in the markets for goods, as it does in financial markets.

For some goods the law works very well. On one day in February 1995, the dollar price of an ounce of gold in Hong Kong, London, Paris, Frankfurt, Zurich and New York ranged from $378.81 to $379.35, a gap of 54 cents, or 0.14%. Gold, however, is homogeneous, easy to transport and traded every day by finance professionals.

The Big Mac index, which The Economist created in 1986 as a lighthearted guide to the law, uses a product that is almost the same in every country. In July 2026 a Big Mac cost 7.30 Swiss francs, or $9.04, in Switzerland, and ¥500, or $3.08, in Japan, about a third of the Swiss price. Some inputs of a Big Mac can be traded across borders, such as the bread and the meat. Others cannot, such as the restaurant itself, the wages of its staff and the local taxes.

## Purchasing Power Parity

If the law of one price held for every good, it would also hold for a basket of goods. This is absolute purchasing power parity (PPP), under which the equilibrium exchange rate is the ratio of the domestic price level $P$ to the foreign price level $P^\ast$.

$$e = \frac{P}{P^\ast}$$

The idea goes back to the philosopher David Hume and the classical economists. Suppose that prices in one country are far below those in the rest of the world. Foreigners buy more of its goods, because the country is cheap, and it buys fewer foreign goods, because its currency buys little abroad. Foreign currency flows in through exports, so domestic prices rise, or the domestic currency appreciates because foreign currency becomes abundant, or both. The process goes on until prices, measured in the same currency, are equal. Exports and imports thus push the domestic and foreign price levels together.

Hume imagined four-fifths of the money of Great Britain destroyed in one night. Prices would fall in proportion, no nation could compete with British goods in foreign markets, and the inflow of money would soon "raise us to the level of all the neighbouring nations".

Absolute price levels are hard to measure, so in practice economists mostly use relative PPP. It does not require equal price levels, only that the exchange rate move with the difference in inflation.

$$\frac{\Delta e}{e} \approx \pi - \pi^\ast$$

The currency of the country with more inflation tends to depreciate, and that of the country with less inflation tends to appreciate. If a country devalues to gain competitiveness, the gain lasts only until its prices rise. A devaluation can work in the short run, but not in the long run.

The real exchange rate $q$ is the price of foreign goods relative to domestic goods, measured in the same currency.

$$q = \frac{e P^\ast}{P}$$

Absolute PPP says that $q = 1$, and relative PPP says that $q$ is constant. A rise in $q$ is a real depreciation, and a fall is a real appreciation. A real effective exchange rate is an average of the real exchange rates of a country against its trading partners, weighted by trade.

## Measuring PPP

Economists use better data than the Big Mac. Bruegel publishes real effective exchange rates, based on consumer prices, for 178 countries. The World Bank publishes PPP conversion factors from the International Comparison Program, whose latest results cover 176 economies in 2021. A PPP conversion factor is the number of units of a currency that buy in the domestic market what one dollar buys in the United States. In 2025, for example, 0.571 euros bought in Spain what one dollar bought in the United States. These factors remove the differences in price levels, so they make output and standards of living comparable across countries.

Indices of real exchange rates, however, have a base year, and the Bruegel indices, for example, equal 100 in 2007. They show how the position of a country has changed since that year, not whether its currency is overvalued against other countries at a given moment, so the conclusion depends on the base year chosen. In the early 1990s the real exchange rate of the Mexican peso appreciated sharply, and at the end of 1994 the peso collapsed. Was the overvaluation clear in advance? Not necessarily. Against a base year in the mid-1980s, when the debt crisis had sunk the real value of the peso, the appreciation looks huge. Against a later base year, it looks much smaller.

## Why Prices Do Not Converge

If arbitrage exists, why does a Big Mac cost almost three times more in Switzerland than in Japan? Several frictions stop prices from converging completely.

- **Transport costs.** For most products they are small, but not for all. Where infrastructure is missing, or destroyed by an earthquake, they can be enormous. A rough measure, the gap between the value of world imports including transport and insurance and the value of world exports without them, was about 10% of the value of world trade in the early 1990s, with large differences across countries.
- **Taxes.** Trade can equalize prices before tax, but not after tax. If the VAT is much higher in one country, the shop price reflects it, and arbitrage cannot remove the difference.
- **Tariffs and non-tariff barriers.** Sanitary controls, technical standards and customs inspections raise the cost of trade a lot, and unevenly across products. A delay at customs can cost an exporter of fruit and vegetables the whole shipment. Some barriers nobody imposes on purpose. A lamp for the 230 volts of most of Europe is useless in the United States, which uses 120 volts, and a car with the steering wheel on the left is hard to sell in the United Kingdom, where traffic keeps to the left and most cars sold have the wheel on the right.
- **Non-tradable goods and services.** A house cannot move to another country, so differences in the price of housing can be huge and last a very long time. Local services also enter the prices of tradable goods. A low rent makes the bread of a bakery cheaper, and the wage of the waiter is part of the price of a beer.
- **Restrictions on competition.** A company with market power can charge a different price in each country, and if barriers stop others from entering the market, nobody can arbitrage the difference. Companies also spend resources to protect themselves from competition, for example by capturing the regulator so that the rules suit them.

These deviations are not a modern phenomenon. Prices of three grains, three dairy products, peas and silver in England and Holland cover the period from the late 13th century to the 20th. The volatility of their deviations from the law of one price, typically 20% or more a year, was no larger in the 20th century than in the 14th, and their persistence hardly changed, through plagues and wars and from sailing ships to steamships. The explanation therefore cannot lie in the institutions of the 20th century.

## The Balassa–Samuelson Effect

Non-tradable goods combined with differences in productivity between countries give the Balassa–Samuelson effect. Rich countries are relatively more productive in tradable goods, such as manufactures, than in non-tradable services, such as a haircut. The world market sets the prices of tradable goods, so high productivity there translates into high wages. Within a country workers can change sector, so a hairdresser must earn a wage close to the wage in the tradable industries, even if they cut hair no faster or better than a hairdresser in a poor country. Services are therefore much dearer in a rich country, and so is its general price level.

The relation is clear when rich and poor countries are put together. In 1990 data for 100 countries, each 1% of extra income per person went with a price level about 0.37% higher, so a country with 10% more income had on average a price level about 3.5% higher. Within rich countries alone, or within poor countries alone, the relation is far weaker.

The classic example over time is Japan, which had the fastest growth of income per person of any major country after World War II. Its real exchange rate against the dollar appreciated steadily from 1960 to 1995. The equilibrium real exchange rate therefore need not be constant. When a country catches up in income, its real exchange rate tends to appreciate. Either its prices rise faster than foreign prices without a nominal depreciation that offsets them, or its currency appreciates in nominal terms, and neither is necessarily a loss of competitiveness.

Part of the inflation differential between Spain and its euro area partners in the years of convergence has been explained this way. From 1999 to 2007, inflation averaged 3.1% a year in Spain and 2.1% in the euro area. Estimates of the early 2000s put the Balassa–Samuelson part of the Spanish gap at about half. Labor productivity in Spanish tradables, however, grew only 1.9% a year from 1999 to 2007, against 3.2% in the euro area, so later studies found little support for the effect. With a fixed exchange rate, the real appreciation could only come through prices.
