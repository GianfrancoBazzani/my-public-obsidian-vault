# Goals and Tools of Monetary Policy

A central bank sets the overnight interest rate and the size of its balance sheet (see [Monetary Policy Implementation](05-monetary-policy-implementation.md)). This note explains what it tries to achieve with these tools, how it decides when to use them, and how it tells the market what it will do. Its decisions then reach the economy through several channels (see [Interest Rate and Credit Channels](13-interest-rate-and-credit-channels.md) and [Transmission Channels and Pass-Through](14-transmission-channels-and-pass-through.md)).

## Goals

### Two Mandates

The Federal Reserve Act tells the Fed to promote "the goals of maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates", in words that the Federal Reserve Reform Act of 1977 added. An economy with maximum employment and stable prices creates the conditions for moderate long-term rates, so the Fed speaks of a dual mandate. In January 2012 the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) stated for the first time that inflation of 2%, measured by the annual change in the price index for personal consumption expenditures (PCE), is most consistent with this mandate.

The ECB has a clearer hierarchy. Under Article 127(1) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, its primary objective is price stability, and only "without prejudice to the objective of price stability" does it support the general economic policies in the Union. Since July 2021 the ECB aims for inflation of 2% over the medium term, measured by the harmonised index of consumer prices (HICP), and it considers deviations below and above the target equally undesirable. From 2003 to 2021 the aim was inflation "below, but close to, 2%". In June 2025 the ECB confirmed the symmetric target.

| | Fed | ECB |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legal basis | Federal Reserve Act, Section 2A, since 1977 | Article 127(1) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union |
| Goals | Maximum employment and stable prices, without a ranking | Price stability first, and support for the general economic policies of the Union only without prejudice to it |
| Inflation target | 2% for the PCE price index over the longer run, since 2012 | 2% for the HICP over the medium term, symmetric, since 2021 |

### Maximum Employment Cannot Be Observed

Central banks do not know which unemployment rate is full employment. Given the institutions of a country, there is a lowest unemployment rate that can last without a rise in wage growth and inflation, which is the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment (NAIRU). Nobody can observe it, and its estimates are very uncertain. One estimate put the US NAIRU of 1990 at 6.2%, with a 95% confidence interval from 5.1% to 7.7%. The median estimate of FOMC participants for the longer-run unemployment rate fell from 5.5% in 2012 to 4.1% in 2020. The FOMC therefore states that the maximum level of employment "is not directly measurable and changes over time", and it sets no fixed goal for employment.

### Track Record

Both central banks missed their targets for years. From 2009 to 2020, US PCE inflation, measured from the fourth quarter of one year to the fourth quarter of the next, was below 2% in every year except 2011. Euro area HICP inflation averaged less than 2% in every year from 2013 to 2020, and only 0.2% in 2015 and 2016. Then inflation went far above the targets. In June 2022 US consumer price inflation reached 9.1%, the highest since 1981, and PCE inflation peaked at 7.2%. In October 2022 euro area HICP inflation reached 10.6%, the highest since the series began in 1997.

In a world of large public debts, price stability can be easier to promise than to deliver. Fiscal dominance is a situation in which monetary policy cannot tighten because of fiscal constraints. The central bank may be under political pressure to keep rates low and so make room for public spending, or a rise in rates may risk a crisis of sovereign debt. In the extreme case, deficits that bond sales cannot finance force the central bank to create money and accept more inflation. The Treasury, and not the central bank, then sets the course of monetary policy.

## Tools

A central bank has three tools to reach its goals.

- **Short-term interest rate.** The central bank steers the overnight rate with the rates of its facilities (see [Monetary Policy Implementation](05-monetary-policy-implementation.md)).
- **Balance sheet.** It buys long-term assets in quantitative easing (QE), and it sells them or lets them mature without reinvestment in quantitative tightening (QT) (see [Modern Money Creation](04-modern-money-creation.md)).
- **Communication.** It tells the public what it thinks and what it plans to do, which is forward guidance.

## Rules or Discretion

A central bank can use its tools with discretion, which means that it decides at each meeting what seems best, or with a rule, which commits it to react in the same way to the same circumstances.

Discretion brings temptations. Lower rates cut unemployment in the short run, so a central bank is tempted to stimulate the economy a little more today at the cost of a little more inflation tomorrow. People expect this, so the economy can end up with high inflation and no gain in employment. A central bank can also give in to fiscal pressure and buy public debt to accommodate the deficits of the government, even when this harms monetary stability in the medium and long run, which is fiscal dominance.

A rule makes the central bank predictable. If the market knows the rule and believes it, each new figure for inflation tells it what the central bank will do, so households, companies and investors can plan better. A credible rule thus promises more stability in financial markets.

### The Taylor Rule

In 1993 John Taylor proposed a simple rule for the federal funds rate.

$$i = \pi + 0.5 (\pi - 2) + 0.5 y + 2$$

- $i$ is the federal funds rate.
- $\pi$ is the inflation of the previous four quarters, measured with the GDP deflator.
- $\pi - 2$ is the deviation of inflation from the target of 2%.
- $y$ is the output gap, the percent deviation of real GDP from its trend, or potential.
- The final 2 is the equilibrium real interest rate, which Taylor set at 2%.

All terms are in percent. Because inflation enters twice, the rate rises by 1.5 points for each point of inflation, so the real rate rises when inflation rises. The rate also rises by half a point for each point of output above potential. If inflation and output are on target, the rate is 4%, or 2% in real terms.

For example, with inflation of 4% and output 2% below potential, the rule gives a rate of 6%.

$$i = 4 + 0.5 \times (4 - 2) + 0.5 \times (-2) + 2 = 4 + 1 - 1 + 2 = 6$$

Taylor derived the rule from research on policy rules, as a recommendation. Yet the Fed had announced no rule, and the rule matched its actual federal funds rate from 1987 to 1992 remarkably well, so it also described what the Fed already did. In the same way, Bagehot's rule of 1873 put into words what the Bank of England had learned in the crises before it (see [Role of Central Banks](02-role-of-central-banks.md)).

## Communication

The third tool is to talk. Few things in central banking have changed more in recent decades. The Fed used to be more opaque, and its decisions sometimes surprised the market, but today it puts great effort into explaining what it thinks.

| Year | Change in the communication of the Fed |
| --- | --- |
| Until 1967 | The record of the policy actions of the FOMC appears only once a year, in the annual report of the Board |
| 1967–1976 | The record appears about 90 days after each meeting, from 1975 after 45 days, and from 1976 a few days after the next meeting |
| 1994 | First announcement of a policy decision right after the meeting, on 4 February |
| 2000 | A statement after every meeting, from February |
| 2005 | Minutes three weeks after each meeting, from 4 January, by a decision of December 2004 |
| 2007 | Projections of FOMC participants four times a year, in the Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) |
| 2011 | Press conferences of the Chair four times a year, from 27 April |
| 2012 | The dot plot, from 25 January |
| 2019 | A press conference after every meeting, from 30 January |

### Forward Guidance

Forward guidance is a tool that central banks use to tell the public about the likely future course of monetary policy. It works for two reasons.

- **Words can replace actions.** If the market believes that the central bank will act, households, companies and investors anticipate the action and do part of the work for it, often before the central bank acts.
- **Long-term rates.** The central bank controls the overnight rate, not long-term rates. Yet long-term rates, which matter for mortgages and most productive investment, depend largely on the expected path of the overnight rate (see [Money Market Spreads](10-money-market-spreads.md)). If the central bank convinces the market that short-term rates will stay low for a long time, long-term rates fall too, without purchases of long-term bonds. Guidance can thus give some of the effects of QE without its costs, which makes it an almost free tool, although it can have costs in the long run.

Studies of FOMC announcements find that surprises in forward guidance moved Treasury yields, corporate bond yields, stock prices and exchange rates by amounts comparable to the effects of changes in the federal funds rate in normal times.

In August 2011, for example, the FOMC said that conditions would likely warrant exceptionally low levels for the federal funds rate "at least through mid-2013". In December 2012 it tied low rates to data instead of a date, at least as long as unemployment stayed above 6.5% and projected inflation no more than half a point above 2%. On 9 June 2022 the ECB said that it intended to raise its key rates by 25 basis points (bp) in July and expected to raise them again in September. The 12-month Euribor had already risen from a monthly average of −0.50% in December 2021 to 0.85% in June 2022, before any rise of the policy rates. In July the ECB raised its rates by 50 bp.

### Fed Watchers

If talking is monetary policy, someone must listen, and a small industry of Fed watchers studies every word of the Fed. Joseph Wang, a former senior trader on the open markets desk of the New York Fed, ranks the channels of the Fed by importance.

| Importance | Channels |
| --- | --- |
| High | FOMC statement, press conference of the Chair, minutes, dot plot |
| Medium | Speeches and interviews of Fed officials, statements of the open markets desk, balance sheet of the Fed |
| Low | Research of Fed economists, surveys |

The channels of low rank still matter, but the channels of high rank matter much more.

- **Statement.** The FOMC, which sets the target for the federal funds rate, publishes its statement right after each meeting. It is among the most important channels, and it is short and quick to read. Since June 2026 it has had only three short paragraphs, of 130 to 172 words in all.
- **Minutes.** The minutes appear three weeks after each meeting and report the discussion without names. Their quantifiers show how widely a view was shared. From the largest group to the smallest, the usual words are "all", "almost all", "most", "many", "some", "several", "a few", "a couple" and "one". "Members" are the voters of the FOMC, and "participants" also include the presidents of the Reserve Banks who do not vote. If most participants see a problem with inflation, for example, rises in rates should follow, as the Taylor rule suggests.
- **Dot plot.** A chart in the SEP in which each dot shows the view of one participant on the appropriate federal funds rate at the end of each of the next few years and in the longer run.

### Credibility

Guidance works only while the central bank is credible. When it loses its credibility, its words carry no weight, and it may have to act much more strongly than if it had never given guidance. The Volcker disinflation of the early 1980s followed several failed attempts to lower inflation over the previous 15 years, and only a long period of very restrictive policy brought inflation down.

### Average Inflation Targeting

Communication can also show a change of strategy. On 27 August 2020 the Fed said that it would seek "inflation that averages 2 percent over time". After periods of inflation persistently below 2%, it would aim for inflation moderately above 2% for some time, so that years of low inflation could be made up with years of high inflation. In July 2021 the ECB took a similar step. Its new symmetric target accepted, near the lower bound of rates, "a transitory period in which inflation is moderately above target".

A cynical reading is that the Fed already expected the inflation that came after 2020. Its own projections do not support this. In September 2020 the median FOMC participant expected PCE inflation of 1.7% in 2021 and 1.8% in 2022, but inflation was 5.7% and 6.1%, from fourth quarter to fourth quarter. On 22 August 2025 the Fed dropped the averaging and returned to flexible inflation targeting. Its Chair explained that "there was nothing intentional or moderate about the inflation that arrived a few months after we announced our 2020 changes to the consensus statement". In June 2025 the ECB kept its symmetric target but dropped the sentence on a transitory period above target.
