# Money Origins and Role

## Money as a Social Technology

Money is a social technology that emerged and developed to solve the problems of barter.

### Barter

Before money, people traded goods directly for other goods. This is barter. A farmer who has wheat and wants shoes must find a shoemaker and give wheat for the shoes.

Barter works in small groups, where people know each other and trade only a few goods. But when the division of labor grows, barter becomes slow and costly. Each barter trade needs three coincidences at once, a coincidence of wants, a coincidence in space, and a coincidence in time.

### Coincidence of Wants

In a barter trade, each person must want what the other person offers. The farmer wants shoes, and the shoemaker must want wheat. This is the double coincidence of wants.

If the shoemaker wants fish and not wheat, the trade does not happen. The farmer must first find a fisherman who wants wheat, trade the wheat for fish, and then trade the fish for the shoes. Each extra step costs time and effort. When the number of goods in the economy increases, the chance to find a person with the exact opposite wants becomes very small.

### Coincidence in Space

The two people must also meet in the same place. If the farmer lives in a village and the shoemaker lives in a town far away, one of them must travel with the goods. Heavy or bulky goods, such as wheat or cattle, are expensive to carry over long distances. Thus, barter limits trade to people who live near each other and to goods that are easy to move.

### Coincidence in Time

The two people must also be ready to trade at the same time. The farmer has wheat only after the harvest, but needs shoes in winter. The shoemaker can need wheat today and have shoes ready only next month.

Many goods also lose their value over time. Wheat rots, cattle get sick, and fish goes bad in a few days. Thus, a person cannot easily keep the value of today's work to buy goods in the future.

### From Barter to Money

People solve these problems with indirect exchange. They accept a good that they do not want to consume, because they know that other people accept it too. This good becomes a medium of exchange, and the most accepted medium of exchange becomes money.

Money removes the three coincidences.

- **Wants.** The farmer sells the wheat to anyone who wants it, and buys the shoes from anyone who sells them. The two trades do not need to happen with the same person.
- **Space.** Money is light and easy to carry, so a person can trade with people far away.
- **Time.** Money keeps its value over time, so a person can sell today and buy in the future.

Which good becomes money, and how, is the subject of the theory of the saleableness of goods.

## Menger's Theory of the Origin of Money

### Theory of the Saleableness of Goods

Carl Menger explained the origin of money with the saleableness of goods, today usually called salability or liquidity. In this theory, goods have different degrees of saleableness, and any theory of money necessarily requires a theory of the saleableness of goods. A good is more saleable when its owner can sell it more easily, at any time, at a price close to its economic price. 

Saleableness depends on four groups of factors.

- **Buyers.** The number of people who want the good, how strongly and how often they want it, and their purchasing power.
- **Quantity.** The available quantity of the good relative to the unsatisfied need for it, and the divisibility of the good.
- **Space.** How widely the need for the good is spread, the cost to transport it relative to its value, the development of transport and commerce, and the reach of organized markets connected by arbitrage.
- **Time.** How stable the need for the good is, its durability, its storage cost, the rate of interest, how often its markets meet, and the development of speculation and forward contracts.

In all four groups, developed markets and speculation increase saleableness, and political and social restrictions on exchange, transport, or storage decrease it.

In barter, traders first exchange a good that is hard to sell for a more saleable one, which brings them closer to the good they want. Others copy them, so the most saleable goods become even more saleable. In the end, one or a few goods, such as precious metals, are accepted by all and become money, without any law or agreement. Money is thus not a different kind of good, only the good with the highest degree of saleableness.

### Bid-Ask Spread

The price to buy a good and the price to sell it at once are two different magnitudes. A person who buys a book or a piece of clothing cannot resell it at once, in the same market, at the same price. This gap, today called the bid-ask spread, measures saleableness. The smaller the gap, the more saleable the good. Grain or cotton sells in almost any quantity at any time near the current price, cloth or silk sells only at a clear discount, and astronomical instruments sell at a large loss. The gap also grows as the time to sell shrinks. A seller who must sell within a short, fixed period accepts a larger discount than one who can wait for the right buyer, and this effect is strongest for goods with few buyers. Money has almost no gap, so its holder can buy any good at its economic price.

### Recursive Effect of Salability

Traders bring less saleable goods to the market to get goods of another kind, so each trader has an interest to first exchange them for more saleable goods. Every such exchange widens the market of the more saleable good, which makes it even more saleable and attracts more traders. Others copy the traders who succeed with this method, and the practice becomes a habit. When a good becomes money, its saleableness increases further and widens the gap with all other goods, until the difference stops being only one of degree and becomes, in a certain sense, absolute.

This gives the holder of money a position of power. The holder of money can buy any good at any time at its economic price. The holder of any other good must first overcome a difficulty, the sale of that good, before reaching the same purpose. This is the main explanation of the superior position of the buyer over the seller, a fact long observed but, before this theory, poorly explained. The Latin maxim *pecuniam habens, habet omnem rem quem vult habere*, "whoever has money has every thing they want to have", sums up this advantage.

### Precious Metals

Many societies used goods such as cattle, skins, salt, or shells as media of exchange. As they advanced, and once they had a sufficient supply, they adopted the precious metals as money, at different times and without any state order or agreement among traders. The reason is that the precious metals rank high in every factor of saleableness. Almost everyone wants them for their use and beauty, and their supply is small relative to this demand. They are homogeneous, very divisible, durable, found in many regions, easy to extract and work, and cheap to store and to transport relative to their value. Their exchange ratio with other goods changes little, and their color and ring make their quality and weight easy to check, so they became the favorite means to store wealth.

### Bottom-Up Emergence of Money

Money did not come from law. It is a social institution, not a state one. A medium of exchange can also be created by law, like other social institutions, but this is neither the only nor the main way in which money arose. The origin of money can only be understood as a spontaneous process, the unplanned result of the individual efforts of the members of a society, who little by little learned to tell apart the different degrees of saleableness of goods.

The state did not make the precious metals money. It later recognized and regulated money, for example with coinage that reduced the risk about the genuineness, weight, and fineness of the metal. In this way the state perfected money and adapted it to the changing needs of commerce, as statute law perfects customary law.
