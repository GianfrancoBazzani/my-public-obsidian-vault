# Interest Rate and Credit Channels

Most economists agree that monetary policy moves real output in the short run, but they agree much less on how it does so. The path from a change in the policy rate to production and employment is a black box. This note opens it with US evidence on what happens after an unexpected rise in the policy rate, and with the credit channel, which explains the facts that the textbook story cannot explain. Three more channels, and the pass-through of the policy rate to the rates of banks, complete the picture (see [Transmission Channels and Pass-Through](14-transmission-channels-and-pass-through.md)).

## The Interest Rate Channel

In the textbook story, the central bank raises the short-term rate, and because prices adjust slowly, the real rate rises too. A higher real rate raises the cost of capital, so spending on durable goods, such as business investment, housing, inventories and consumer durables, falls, and production falls with it. This is the interest rate channel, also called the cost-of-capital channel.

## What Happens After a Rise in the Policy Rate

A vector autoregression (VAR) on US monthly data from January 1965 to December 1993 traces the response of the economy to an unexpected rise in the federal funds rate. Four facts stand out.

1. **The rise in the rate is short, but the fall in output and prices lasts.** The funds rate begins to fall sharply after three to four months. Nine to twelve months after the shock it keeps a quarter or less of its initial rise, and after two years it is back to trend. Real GDP begins to fall about four months after the shock and reaches its lowest point after about two years. The price level does not move for about a year and then falls.
2. **Final demand falls first.** Production follows with a lag, so inventories rise for the first months. When inventories then fall, they account for a large part of the decline in GDP.
3. **Housing falls earliest and most.** Residential investment drops sharply and accounts for a large part of the initial fall in final demand. Consumer spending on durables and nondurables follows close behind. Nondurables fall much less in percent, but they contribute about as much as durables because their share of the economy is larger.
4. **Business investment falls last.** Business fixed investment falls later than housing, consumer durables and production, mostly between 6 and 24 months after the shock. Equipment accounts for nearly all of the fall, and business structures hardly respond.

## Three Puzzles

The slow response of prices and the fast response of final demand fit the textbook story. Three other facts do not fit it.

- **Magnitude.** Small moves in market interest rates have large effects on the economy. Yet studies of investment, housing and inventories rarely find a strong effect of the cost of capital, and variables such as past sales and cash flow explain spending better.
- **Timing.** The rise in the funds rate is almost gone 8 to 9 months after the shock. Yet business investment falls mostly between 6 and 24 months after the shock, and inventories begin to fall only when the rate is already falling back. Spending falls when its supposed cause has already disappeared.
- **Composition.** Monetary policy moves short-term rates most, so it should affect short-lived assets most, such as some inventories and consumer durables. Yet its fastest and strongest effect, in percent, is on housing, one of the most long-lived assets. Business structures, which are also long-lived, hardly respond.

## The External Finance Premium

The credit channel adds the frictions of credit markets to the story. A lender knows less than the borrower about the quality of a loan, and every borrower tries to look as creditworthy as possible. The lender must look behind this make-up, and it bears the loss if the borrower defaults. Because of this asymmetric information and the cost of enforcing contracts, external funds, such as a loan or an issue of bonds or shares, cost more than internal funds, such as retained earnings. The difference is the external finance premium. It covers the cost to the lender of evaluation, monitoring and collection, a "lemons" premium for the better information of the borrower, and the cost of the covenants and collateral that limit moral hazard. In a market without frictions, arbitrage would make the premium zero.

A change in monetary policy moves the external finance premium in the same direction as market rates. A tightening thus raises the total cost of borrowing by more than the rise in market rates. The credit channel is therefore not a separate channel, but a mechanism that amplifies and extends the interest rate channel. It works in two ways, through the balance sheets of borrowers and through the supply of bank loans.

## The Balance Sheet Channel

The more net worth a borrower has, in liquid assets and marketable collateral, the smaller its external finance premium. A borrower with a strong balance sheet can finance more of its project itself or post more collateral, so the lender takes less risk. Down payments, collateral and minimum financial ratios in loan contracts all rest on this idea.

A rise in rates weakens the balance sheets of borrowers in three ways.

- **Interest.** A borrower with short-term or floating-rate debt pays more interest, so its net cash flow falls.
- **Collateral.** Asset prices fall, so the collateral that the borrower can post is worth less.
- **Revenue.** Customers spend less, so revenue falls, while quasi-fixed costs, such as wages and interest, do not fall in the short run.

The coverage ratio of nonfinancial companies is a simple measure of this pressure.

$$\text{Coverage ratio} = \frac{\text{Interest payments}}{\text{Interest payments} + \text{Profits}}$$

The coverage ratio moves almost in step with the federal funds rate. A rise in the funds rate raises the numerator through interest payments, and it lowers profits in the denominator through weaker demand. Short-term debt also rises after a tightening, mostly to finance the build-up of inventories, which raises interest payments further. In US quarterly data from 1965 to 1994, something over 40% of the short-run fall in corporate profits after a tightening came from higher interest payments. The rest came from revenue that fell faster than quasi-fixed costs, such as employee compensation.

The squeeze on cash flow peaks 6 to 9 months after the tightening, when output, inventories and investment begin to fall. Interest payments stay high for several more quarters, although short-term rates are already falling, because companies carry more short-term debt. This answers the puzzle of timing. Market rates fall, but balance sheets keep getting weaker, so the external finance premium, and with it the cost of capital in a broad sense, keeps rising.

### Large and Small Firms

The effect of the squeeze depends on how easily a company can borrow to bridge the fall in its cash flow. After a tightening, large manufacturing firms, which can issue commercial paper (see [Unsecured Money Market](08-unsecured-money-market.md)) and have other sources of short-term credit, borrow more, and their inventories grow, so they can keep production and employment for a time. Small firms, with less access to credit, cannot borrow more, so they cut inventories, prices and production. In US manufacturing, small firms made about 30% of sales, but after a tightening they accounted for 55% to 60% of the fall in sales and for a disproportionate share of the fall in inventories. The differences appear mainly in recessions and periods of tight money. They are average tendencies, because a small firm with little debt can resist a tightening better than a large firm with too much debt.

## The Bank Lending Channel

Banks specialize in lending to borrowers whose quality is hard to see from outside, such as small and medium-sized firms. If a bank cuts its loans, these borrowers are not shut off from credit, but they must pay the cost of finding a new lender and building a new relationship. A smaller supply of bank loans therefore raises their external finance premium.

In the traditional view, open market sales drain reserves, and with them deposits, from the banks, so the banks must lend less. This view fitted the US before about 1980, when Regulation Q capped deposit rates (see [Unsecured Money Market](08-unsecured-money-market.md)) and reserve requirements also applied to large CDs. Since then, banks can replace lost deposits with managed liabilities, such as large CDs, and today central banks set the price of reserves, not their quantity (see [Modern Money Creation](04-modern-money-creation.md)).

The channel needs only that this replacement costs more. Deposit insurance covers retail deposits, so all banks compete for them on equal terms. Large CDs and other wholesale funding are only partly insured or not insured at all, so lenders must evaluate and monitor the bank. Small and poorly capitalized banks pay more for this funding, or cannot issue large CDs at all. In past US tightenings, the rate on bank CDs rose more than the Treasury bill rate, and the prime rate of bank loans rose more than safe rates. In US bank data from 1976 to 1993, the lending of banks with less liquid balance sheets, that is, with fewer securities relative to assets, reacted most to monetary policy, and mostly at the smaller banks. Small banks also lend most to small firms. In 2019, US community banks held 15% of the loans of the banking industry but 36% of its loans to small businesses. A tightening thus reaches small firms through both channels.

The two channels are hard to separate in the data, because a tightening weakens the balance sheets of borrowers and of banks at the same time. The evidence for a credit channel in general is stronger than the evidence for each channel alone.

## Measuring the Credit Channel

The volume of credit is a poor measure of the credit channel, because it depends on both supply and demand, as the bank lending survey shows (see [Modern Money Creation](04-modern-money-creation.md)). Right after a tightening, companies borrow more to finance the inventories that they cannot sell, and households may borrow to cover a fall in income. Some kinds of credit can thus grow while credit conditions get worse. The theory does not say that borrowers borrow less, only that they borrow and spend less than they would in a market without frictions. Credit conditions are best measured by prices, such as the external finance premium and the spreads between money market rates (see [Money Market Spreads](10-money-market-spreads.md)), not by quantities.

## How the Credit Channel Answers the Puzzles

| Puzzle | Answer of the credit channel |
| --- | --- |
| Magnitude | Measures of the cost of capital leave out the external finance premium, which rises with the policy rate and amplifies its effect. |
| Timing | Balance sheets keep getting weaker after market rates fall, because cash flow stays squeezed and short-term debt is higher, so the premium keeps rising. |
| Composition | Housing depends on the balance sheets of households through down payments, closing costs and the ratio of mortgage payments to income, the household counterpart of the coverage ratio, which rises with the funds rate. Before 1980, outflows of deposits from banks under Regulation Q also cut mortgage credit. |
