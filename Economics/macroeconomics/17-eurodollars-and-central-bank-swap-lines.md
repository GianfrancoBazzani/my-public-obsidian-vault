# Eurodollars and Central Bank Swap Lines

A very large amount of dollars is created outside the United States, by banks that have no account at the Fed and no direct access to it. This note explains where these dollars come from, how banks without a Fed account settle them, and who supplies dollars when they become scarce.

## Eurodollars

Eurodollars are dollar deposits at banks outside the United States, including the foreign branches of US banks, and outside US banking regulation. They got their name because at first they were held almost only in Europe. The London market dates from mid-1955. Today eurodollars are also held in places such as the Bahamas, Bahrain, Canada, the Cayman Islands, Hong Kong, Japan, Panama and Singapore. Among the first clients were the governments of the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, which feared confiscation if they held their dollars in the United States, and the first eurodollar deposits were reportedly placed by communist-owned banks in the West.

## Growth

Regulation Q helped the market grow in the 1960s (see [Unsecured Money Market](08-unsecured-money-market.md)). It capped the rates that US banks could pay on deposits, but not the rates of their branches abroad. When market rates rose above the ceilings, in 1966 and 1969, US banks borrowed large amounts of eurodollars, mainly through their branches in London. In October 1969 the Fed put a 10% reserve requirement on such borrowing above its level of May 1969. In the 1970s, the market grew mainly with the dollars of oil exporters, after the oil price rises of 1973 and of the late 1970s.

Before 2008, the market grew again with the dollar assets of European banks, which ranged from US Treasury and agency securities to structured finance products, such as securities backed by US mortgages. The banks funded these long-term assets with short-term dollars. By mid-2007 their funding gap, the short-term dollar funding that they needed to roll over, was $1.0 trillion to $1.2 trillion, which they borrowed from other banks, from central banks and through foreign exchange swaps that turned their euros into dollars. With the commercial paper and certificates of deposit that their New York branches sold to US money market funds, the gap was $2.0 trillion to $2.2 trillion (see [Unsecured Money Market](08-unsecured-money-market.md)).

## The Market Today

Today the typical eurodollar loan involves no US resident at all, for example a Japanese bank that lends dollars to a Korean company. Eurodollar markets mostly bring together investors and borrowers outside the United States. At the end of June 2026, non-banks outside the United States owed $15.13 trillion in dollars, $6.94 trillion in bank loans and $8.20 trillion in debt securities, 8.0% more than a year earlier. This is about 7.9 times the GDP of Spain in 2025, which was about $1.91 trillion. Dollar banking outside the United States is about half the size of dollar banking inside it, so it is about a third of all dollar banking in the world.

## Why the World Wants Dollars

- **Safety.** The dollar is the main reserve currency and a safe haven. It was 56.70% of the allocated foreign exchange reserves of central banks in the second quarter of 2026.
- **Trade.** Most commodities are priced in dollars, and much trade between countries that do not use the dollar is invoiced and paid in dollars. About 40% of world exports are invoiced in dollars. From 1999 to 2019, the share was 96% in the Americas and 74% in the Asia-Pacific region.
- **Cost.** A company can often borrow more cheaply in dollars than in the currency of its own country.
- **Liquidity.** No debt market is deeper than that of the United States, with $32.15 trillion of marketable Treasury debt at the end of October 2026, and no currency is more widely accepted. The dollar was on one side of 89% of all foreign exchange trades in April 2025.

## Dollars Without a Fed Account

A bank outside the United States creates dollars as any bank creates money, by lending (see [Modern Money Creation](04-modern-money-creation.md)). The difference is in how it settles the payments that leave or enter it, because no central bank acts as its bank of banks or clearing house in dollars. It settles through a correspondent bank, which is a bank that holds the deposits of other banks and makes payments for them.

Suppose that a small European bank lends $100 to a company and credits a deposit of $100 to the company. The company then pays a supplier that has an account at a US bank. The European bank has no reserves at the Fed, but it has a deposit at a large US bank. It asks that bank to pay for it. The correspondent sends $100 of reserves to the bank of the supplier and reduces the deposit of the European bank by $100.

| Step | European bank | US correspondent bank | Bank of the supplier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Loan | Loan +100, deposit of the company +100 | No change | No change |
| Payment | Deposit at the correspondent −100, deposit of the company −100 | Reserves −100, deposit of the European bank −100 | Reserves +100, deposit of the supplier +100 |

For the European bank, its deposit at the correspondent plays the role of reserves. The eurodollar system is thus a fractional reserve system built on top of another fractional reserve system. In the hierarchy of money, reserves at the Fed are at the top, deposits at US banks are below them, and eurodollars are below those deposits (see [Credit and Currency](03-credit-and-currency.md)). In the money matrix, eurodollars are private money with no explicit public backstop (see [Cash Pools and Shadow Money](15-cash-pools-and-shadow-money.md)).

## Who Backs the Eurodollar

In normal times nobody backs the eurodollar explicitly. A US bank in trouble can borrow from the discount window of the Fed. Since the International Banking Act of 1978, the US branches and agencies of foreign banks that hold reserves can borrow from it too. A foreign bank with no office in the United States cannot. Eurodollar banks are therefore exposed to runs. If deposits leave fast, a bank that issued eurodollars must raise the rate that it pays on its deposits to whatever the market asks, or fail for lack of liquidity.

The answer to this problem has been the central bank swap line. In a swap line, the Fed sells dollars to a foreign central bank, such as the ECB, for its currency at the market exchange rate. The two agree to reverse the trade on a later date at the same exchange rate, and the foreign central bank then pays interest to the Fed. The Fed therefore bears no exchange rate risk. The foreign central bank lends the dollars to its banks and bears the credit risk of these loans, and the Fed is not a party to them.

| Date | Facility |
| --- | --- |
| 31 October 2013 | Standing swap lines with the Bank of Canada, the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan, the ECB and the Swiss National Bank |
| 19 March 2020 | Temporary swap lines with the central banks of Australia, Brazil, Denmark, Korea, Mexico, New Zealand, Norway, Singapore and Sweden, extended to 31 December 2021 |
| 31 March 2020 | FIMA repo facility, through which foreign official institutions borrow dollars overnight against the Treasuries that they hold at the New York Fed, standing since 28 July 2021 |

The swaps outstanding peaked at about $583 billion on 17 December 2008 and at $448.9 billion on 27 May 2020 (see [Unsecured Money Market](08-unsecured-money-market.md)).

The Fed has thus become the central bank of the world, perhaps without intending to. The hierarchy of money appears again, now between countries. A club of central banks has permanent access to the issuer of the international reserve currency. A periphery can create dollar deposits whenever it wants, but has only limited access to the ultimate means of settlement, the dollars that the Fed creates. The Fed can also refuse to open a swap line. In 1971 the US Treasury Secretary, John Connally, reportedly told European officials that "the dollar is our currency, but your problem."

In October 2025, the US Treasury, not the Fed, signed a $20 billion swap with the Central Bank of the Argentine Republic, through its Exchange Stabilization Fund. It was signed on 18 October and announced on 20 October. The Argentine central bank drew $2.5 billion in October and repaid it in December 2025, with $17.7 million of interest, and the line stayed in place. The swap was politically controversial. Some senators proposed a bill to stop the support while US soybean farmers suffered, and the Treasury Secretary replied that it was not a bailout. Who backs the eurodollar is therefore not always clear.

Eurodollars carry the dollar outside the United States but keep it inside banks. Stablecoins carry it outside both (see [Stablecoins and Tokenized Deposits](18-stablecoins-and-tokenized-deposits.md)).
