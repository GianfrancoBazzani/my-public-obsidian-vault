# Balance of Payments and the Monetary Approach

The exchange rate can be seen as the relative price of two baskets of goods (see [Exchange Rates and Purchasing Power Parity](19-exchange-rates-and-purchasing-power-parity.md)). Above all, however, it is the price of one currency in terms of another, and a currency is a liability of its issuer (see [Credit and Currency](03-credit-and-currency.md)). It therefore makes sense to look at money and at the institution that issues it. This note does so in two ways, through the reserves that back a currency and through the monetary approach.

## The Balance of Payments

The balance of payments summarizes the transactions between the residents of a country and nonresidents during a period. Its two large accounts are these.

- **Current account.** Mostly trade in goods and services, plus income and transfers from and to abroad.
- **Financial account.** Changes in financial assets and liabilities, such as investments and loans, including the international reserves of the central bank.

A third, small account, the capital account, records capital transfers.

The two large accounts are connected. A country with a trade deficit, which buys more abroad than it sells, can finance it by borrowing abroad, which appears as a capital inflow in the financial account. If the deficit persists and nobody finances it, the country loses reserves. In the opposite case, a country with a current account surplus that does not invest abroad what it earns accumulates international reserves.

## Reserves and the Exchange Rate

Seen from the balance of payments, the exchange rate depends on the quantity of domestic money relative to the international reserves behind it. When reserves come in, each unit of domestic money has more foreign currency behind it, so the exchange rate falls and the currency appreciates. When reserves go out, the opposite happens. Surpluses in the balance of payments therefore go with appreciations, and deficits with depreciations.

The balance sheet of the central bank shows this link.

| Assets | Liabilities |
| --- | --- |
| International reserves, R | Notes and the reserves of banks, M |

The ratio $M/R$ behaves like an exchange rate, in units of domestic money per unit of foreign assets. Its inverse, $R/M$, is the amount of foreign assets behind each unit of domestic money.

## The Backing Theory

This is the backing theory of money. The value of a currency reflects the assets of its issuer, as the value of a share reflects the assets of the company behind it. What matters is not only how much money the central bank issues but what it takes in exchange. If it issues money to buy solid assets that do not lose value, each new unit is backed as well as the old ones, or better, and the currency keeps its value. If it issues money to finance a heavily indebted state whose solvency is in serious doubt, the currency has worse assets behind it, and its value tends to fall. A bad asset shows in the value of the liability, which here is the domestic currency. Backed money thus holds its value as long as its issuer stays solvent.

## Fixed Exchange Rates

A fixed exchange rate is a kind of par between two currencies, and a par is a price that someone must defend (see [Credit and Currency](03-credit-and-currency.md)). The central bank defends it with its international reserves, whether gold, dollars or other foreign assets. If the domestic currency loses value through inflation while the exchange rate stays fixed, the inflation drains the reserves, because people exchange domestic money for foreign money to buy abroad, where goods are now cheaper. Only capital controls and restrictions on trade can then stop the drain. Without them, the reserves run out, the central bank can no longer defend the parity, and a crisis in the balance of payments follows.

## Argentina, 1991–2002

In 1991 Argentina passed the Convertibility Law. From 1 April 1991 the austral was convertible at 10,000 per dollar, and from 1 January 1992 the new peso replaced it at one peso per dollar. The freely available reserves of the central bank, in gold and foreign currency, had to equal at least 100% of the monetary base, which is currency in circulation plus the sight deposits of the banks at the central bank. The system worked while this backing was credible.

The rules, however, let part of the backing be bonds of the Argentine government itself, payable in dollars and valued at market prices. From 1992 the charter of the central bank allowed such bonds to make up to one third of the reserves, and in mid-2001 a change to the charter reduced the required backing further. When serious doubts arose about whether Argentina could pay its debt, too few dollars, or assets of equal value, remained to hold the peso at one to one. In January 2002 Argentina abandoned convertibility. An official rate of 1.40 pesos per dollar applied for about a month to the public sector and most trade transactions, and from 11 February the peso floated. On 25 June 2002 a dollar cost 3.90 pesos, and at the end of the year it cost 3.37.

## The Monetary Approach

The monetary approach to the exchange rate, developed at the start of floating exchange rates in the 1970s, builds the exchange rate in three steps.

1. The price level of each country is set by its money supply relative to its demand for money.
2. The demand for money rises with income and falls with the interest rate, which is the opportunity cost of holding money.
3. PPP turns the two price levels into an exchange rate.

With $m$ and $y$ the logarithms of the money supply and of real income, the three steps give the exchange rate $e$.

$$\ln e = (m - m^\ast) - \phi (y - y^\ast) + \lambda (i - i^\ast)$$

Here $\phi$ and $\lambda$ measure how strongly the demand for money responds to income and to the interest rate. The exchange rate is thus the relative price of two monies, and it moves with the supply of and the demand for each. Three predictions follow.

- **Money.** If a country prints more money than another, its currency depreciates in the same proportion.
- **Growth.** If a country grows faster, it demands more money, and its currency appreciates.
- **Interest rate.** If the interest rate rises because markets expect more inflation, people want to hold less money, because holding it costs more. Prices rise, and the currency depreciates.

The last prediction is the opposite of interest rate parity, in which a higher interest rate appreciates the currency (see [Interest Rate Parity and Overshooting](21-interest-rate-parity-and-overshooting.md)).

## Two Sides of One Balance Sheet

The backing theory and the monetary approach look at the same balance sheet of the central bank from different sides. The monetary approach looks at the liabilities, how much money exists and how much of it people want to hold. The backing theory looks at the assets, what stands behind that money.
