# Cash Pools and Shadow Money

A household keeps its cash in an insured bank deposit that it can spend at once. For the largest holders of cash, such as global companies, asset managers and central banks, the problem is much harder. This note explains where they keep their cash, and why the instruments that they buy are a kind of money that the usual measures of money leave out.

## Cash Pools

Zoltan Pozsar calls these large, centrally managed short-term balances institutional cash pools. He separates four groups.

1. The liquidity tranche of the foreign exchange reserves of central banks.
2. The cash balances of global companies.
3. The centrally managed cash of institutional investors and the largest asset managers.
4. The accounts in which securities lenders reinvest the cash that they receive as collateral.

At the end of 2013, cash pools managed at least $6 trillion. A balance had to be at least $1 billion to count as a cash pool, and the average was about $10 billion. In 2007 the average was $15 billion for a cash-rich company, $20 billion for a large asset manager and $75 billion for a securities lender. No later estimate of the total uses the same method. One part of it, the institutional share of US money market funds, was $4.87 trillion of their $7.98 trillion of assets in the week to 24 November 2026.

## Why Not a Bank Deposit

A cash pool is a treasury, not an investment, so it wants its cash back at par and on demand, and it puts safety before return. A bank deposit makes the same promise, but for a cash pool it is a poor choice.

- **No insurance.** The FDIC insures $250,000 per depositor, per bank and per ownership category, and EU deposit guarantee schemes cover €100,000 per depositor and per bank. A balance of $10 billion is almost all uninsured. In the EU, the deposits of financial institutions, investment funds, pension funds, insurers and public authorities have no cover at all, so many cash pools are uninsured from the first euro.
- **An unsecured and undiversified loan.** An uninsured deposit is a claim on a single bank with no collateral. Even before 2008, with a limit of $100,000, there were not enough banks to spread an average cash pool in insured amounts.
- **No practical alternative in cash.** Nobody can hold billions of dollars in banknotes.
- **No account at the central bank.** Companies and asset managers cannot hold reserves at the Fed, because the Fed keeps reserve accounts for banks and a few other institutions.

The textbook measures of money therefore hardly concern cash pools. M2 includes M1, time deposits under $100,000 and the balances of retail money market funds, but not the shares of institutional funds or repos. The Fed stopped M3, which added large time deposits, institutional money market funds, repos and eurodollars, in March 2006. In the words of Pozsar, "for institutional cash pools, money begins where M2 ends."

Cash pools buy insured deposit alternatives instead, which are safe, short-term and liquid instruments. Pozsar ranks them by safety, with Treasury bills first, then overnight repos backed by Treasuries, shares of government money market funds and shares of prime funds, and last, repos backed by private securities and uninsured deposits. Repos with dealers and shares of money market funds are the two main ones that the private sector issues (see [Money Market Funds, Dealers and Repo](06-money-market-funds-dealers-and-repo.md)). From 2003 to 2008, the demand of cash pools for these instruments exceeded the supply of short-term government-guaranteed instruments that foreign official investors did not hold by at least $1.5 trillion, and the shadow banking system grew to fill the gap.

## Shadow Banking

The word shadow suggests something hidden or illegal. Perry Mehrling and his coauthors define shadow banking simply as "money market funding of capital market lending", sometimes on the balance sheets of banks and sometimes on other balance sheets. An institution buys long-term securities, such as bonds, and funds them with liabilities that the holder can withdraw at once, such as repos or money market fund shares. Like a bank, it transforms maturities, but no deposit insurance and no discount window stand behind it. Pozsar calls the repos and fund shares that it issues shadow money, and cash pools are their main buyers.

## The Money Matrix

Pozsar classifies money claims by two questions. Are the assets behind a claim public or private, and is the backstop that protects its holder public or private?

| | Public backstop | Private backstop |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Public assets** | Public money. Currency and reserves of the Fed, and Treasury bills | Public shadow money. Repos backed by Treasuries, and shares of government money market funds |
| **Private assets** | Insured money. Insured deposits | Private shadow money. Repos backed by private securities, shares of prime money market funds, and uninsured deposits |

An insured deposit is backed by the private loans of a bank, but deposit insurance and the discount window of the central bank protect it. Repos and fund shares backed by Treasuries are the opposite case. Their assets are public, but no explicit public promise protects their holders. Above the insurance limit, a deposit loses its public backstop and falls to the same corner as a repo backed by corporate bonds, the lowest of the matrix.

Silicon Valley Bank shows the difference. At the end of 2022, 94% of its deposits were uninsured, against 41% at other large banks, and companies backed by venture capital held more than half of them. These depositors ran within hours in March 2023 (see [Matched Book and the September 2019 Repo Spike](07-matched-book-and-the-september-2019-repo-spike.md)). For a cash pool, a repo backed by Treasuries is much safer than such a deposit, because if the dealer fails, the lender keeps the bonds and can sell them at once.

## The Size of Shadow Money

In the third quarter of 2013, the shadow banking system issued more than $3.8 trillion of overnight claims at par and on demand, $800 billion more than the $3 trillion of demand deposits of retail and wholesale banks. With term repos and other term claims, the core of the shadow banking system was just under $5 trillion, down from a peak of over $8 trillion in the second quarter of 2008. M2 includes the shares of retail money market funds but not institutional shares or repos. A measure that stops at M2 thus misses a large part of the money of the system, and the part that fell most in the crisis.

The holders of shadow money also make it less stable than deposits. In the words of Pozsar, a system funded by "a relatively low number of well informed, very large and uninsured institutional cash pools is bound to be much less stable than one funded by a very large number of uninformed, small and insured depositors."

Cash pools are only one side of the shadow banking system. The investors that borrow their cash, and the chains of collateral that link the two sides, are the subject of [Collateral Chains and Liquidity Puts](16-collateral-chains-and-liquidity-puts.md).
