# Carry Trade and the Global Financial Cycle

Under uncovered interest parity, what an investor gains in the interest rate it loses in the exchange rate (see [Interest Rate Parity and Overshooting](21-interest-rate-parity-and-overshooting.md)). If the dollar pays 4% and the euro 2%, the market must expect the dollar to depreciate by about 2%. Covered parity says that the forward rate differs from the spot rate by exactly this differential, so uncovered parity also implies that the forward rate is the best forecast of the future spot rate. The data show the opposite.

## The Forward Premium Puzzle

Eugene Fama (1984) tested this with a simple regression for nine currencies against the dollar, with data at intervals of four weeks from August 1973 to December 1982. He regressed the change in the spot rate $s$ on the forward premium, the gap between the forward rate $f$ and the spot rate, all in logarithms.

$$s_{t+1} - s_t = a + b (f_t - s_t) + \varepsilon_{t+1}$$

If uncovered parity held, the slope $b$ would be 1, and what an investor gains in the interest differential it would lose in the depreciation of the currency. All nine slopes were negative. Currencies with high interest rates did not depreciate, as the theory says, but tended to appreciate. This is the forward premium puzzle, and studies of the following decades keep finding it.

Fama read the result as a premium in the forward rate. The forward premium is the sum of this premium and of the expected change in the spot rate, and a negative slope requires that the premium vary more than the expected change and move in the opposite direction. Fama admitted that "a good story for negative covariation" between the two "is difficult to tell".

## The Carry Trade

If uncovered parity fails in this way, one strategy should make money, which is the carry trade. An investor borrows in the currency with the low interest rate, invests in the currency with the high rate and keeps the difference. If the currency with the high rate does not depreciate, or even appreciates, the investor gains twice. Over decades the carry trade has made money on average. From 1986 to 2006, carry trades in eight currencies against the dollar were profitable on average, but they had crash risk.

Traders say that exchange rates go up by the stairs and down by the elevator. The carry trade earns a small, positive return for a long time and can lose a lot at once, so its timing is crucial.

## The Yen in August 2024

For years, borrowing in yen cost almost nothing, while the dollar paid more than 5%, with the target range of the Fed at 5.25% to 5.50% in July 2024. Many investors therefore borrowed yen to buy assets in dollars. Going into August 2024, the BIS put these carry trades at roughly ¥40 trillion, or $250 billion, an estimate that it considered low.

On 31 July 2024 the Bank of Japan raised its policy rate from a range of 0% to 0.1% to about 0.25%. The rise was tiny but rare, and the yen began to appreciate, the more so after weak data on the US labor market on 2 August. Those who owed yen began to lose money and had to close their positions. To repay their debts they bought yen, which pushed the yen up further. From 10 July to 5 August the yen appreciated by 13.5% against the dollar, from 161.47 to 142.24 yen per dollar, computed from the reference rates of the ECB. With a carry of about 5% a year, this took away more than two years of carry in less than a month. On 5 August the Nikkei 225 index of Japanese shares fell by 12.4% in one day, a fall that only the crash of October 1987 exceeded.

## The Price of a Balance Sheet

The matched book of a dealer can rest on the unmatched, speculative position of someone else, who takes the exchange rate risk that the dealers do not want (see [Foreign Exchange as a Funding Market](22-foreign-exchange-as-a-funding-market.md)). Here that someone is the carry trader. If the forward rate equaled the expected spot rate, the carry trader would gain nothing, and nobody would do it. Uncovered parity is thus incompatible with a system in which liquidity is limited and has a price. From this point of view, the gap between the forward rate and the expected spot rate is not a market error. It is the price paid to whoever puts up a balance sheet to bear the risk.

This also explains the elevator. While balance sheet capacity exists, carry trades grow and earn money little by little. When the capacity shrinks or runs out, or an unexpected shock arrives, such as a small rise in Japanese rates or a fast move of the dollar, all the speculators must close their positions at the same time, and nobody is on the other side.

## Dilemma, Not Trilemma

The trilemma of international finance says that, with free movement of capital, a country with a fixed exchange rate imports the monetary policy of the center country, so it can have an independent monetary policy only if its exchange rate floats. Hélène Rey (2013) argues that a floating rate is not enough. A global financial cycle moves capital flows, leverage, credit growth and asset prices across countries together. In her words, they "dance largely to the same tune", and they move with the VIX, the volatility of the S&P 500 index of US shares that options imply for the next 30 days, often called the fear gauge. In 858 prices of risky assets on five continents, one global factor explains about a quarter of the variance, and this factor moves closely with the VIX, in the opposite direction.

One driver of the cycle is the monetary policy of the United States, which works through the leverage of global banks and the flows of credit. When the federal funds rate falls, the VIX falls after about five quarters, and the leverage of European banks and gross credit flows rise. A tighter US policy does the opposite. It reduces the cross-border flows of bank capital and the leverage of international banks, and the dollar appreciates.

With free movement of capital, the global cycle therefore drags a country along even if its exchange rate floats. The shock does not stop at a depreciation of the currency but reaches the whole structure of funding of the country. For Rey, "independent monetary policies are possible if and only if the capital account is managed, directly or indirectly, regardless of the exchange-rate regime". In the present terms of the balance of payments, this is the financial account (see [Balance of Payments and the Monetary Approach](20-balance-of-payments-and-the-monetary-approach.md)). Her options include targeted capital controls and macroprudential policies that limit credit growth and leverage during the upturn of the cycle.

When the Fed tightens, for example, the dollar strengthens. The balance sheets of those who owe dollars worsen, global banks have less balance sheet capacity and lend fewer dollars, and the basis widens (see [Cross-Currency Basis](24-cross-currency-basis.md)). Emerging economies feel the shock whether they like it or not. This is the hierarchy of money once more (see [International Hierarchy of Money](23-international-hierarchy-of-money.md)). Those at the bottom do not set the conditions but take them.
